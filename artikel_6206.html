Even with a strong balance sheet, Ryanair is squeezing pay and hiring as its cheap fuel hedges run out. If the strongest airline in Europe is bracing for a difficult 2027, weaker rivals face a much harder winter.

Halloween is over, but for Europe's airlines the scariest weeks may still lie ahead. Ryanair, the continent's largest carrier, warned Monday of pay freezes, slower recruitment and AI efficiencies as it reported a 15% drop in half-year profits and braced for a €2 billion ($2.24bn) spike in its annual fuel bill in the group's fiscal 2027/28.

The low-cost giant is also anticipating potential industrial action next spring, with group CEO Michael O'Leary suggesting that a pilot strike could actually reduce costs.

"It would be helpful to us if there were strikes, I think, in April or May of next year," O'Leary told analysts on Monday's earnings call. "From a financial point of view ... in the short term, they would save us money."

Ryanair reported a 15% drop in half-year profit after tax to €2.1 billion ($2.35bn) and warned of a €2 billion ($2.24bn) increase in its annual fuel bill in fiscal 2027/28, as the low-cost fuel hedges that have shielded it begin expiring next spring. Group CEO Michael O'Leary said covering that cost cliff through ticket prices alone would require fares to rise about 20%, or €10 per passenger, and that he doesn't expect fares to rise that far — so Ryanair will attack costs instead. The measures include pay freezes for pilot groups in Dublin, the UK, Spain, and Portugal that refuse to settle new collective agreements early, postponed pilot recruitment and training this winter, lower crewing ratios, cheaper airport deals, and greater use of AI in operations and back-office work. O'Leary told analysts that strikes in April or May would save the airline money in the short term, given fuel prices near record highs. The hard line on pay sits alongside a new €800 million ($896m) share buyback starting in February 2027, a contrast an analyst raised directly on the call. Ryanair now expects 214 million passengers in the year to March 2027, down from an earlier 216 million target, with roughly 3% growth the following year — unusually slow for a carrier targeting 300 million passengers by 2034.
