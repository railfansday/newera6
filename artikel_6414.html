Corals have a secret weapon against ocean heat

📅 03 November 2026 | Sumber: Baca Asli

Scientists discovered that bacteria living inside corals produce hormones that help regulate their immune systems during extreme ocean heat.

Coral reefs are disappearing at an alarming rate as ocean temperatures climb.

Scientists understand how extreme heat causes coral bleaching, but one question has remained difficult to answer: Why do some corals withstand heat that devastates others?

A study has found part of the answer in an unexpected place.

Bacteria living inside the coral tissue produce steroid hormones – including testosterone and progesterone – that help the coral’s immune system survive the heat.

The work was led by scientists from the New York University Abu Dhabi.

First author Amin Mohamed and senior author Shady Amin led a team combining field sampling, genomics, metabolomics, and controlled lab experiments.

Their subject was a group of bacteria called Endozoicomonas, one of the most common coral-associated bacteria on earth.

Everyone studying coral health knew it mattered, but nobody had figured out exactly why.

The Arabian Gulf is one of the hottest seas on earth, regularly exceeding 36°C at the surface.

The team sampled Acropora pharaonis corals along a natural temperature gradient there – healthy colonies at 27°C, 32°C, and 34°C, plus corals showing signs of white syndrome disease.

They extracted metabolites and DNA from the coral tissue for analysis.

Most steroid precursors behaved as expected: their abundance fell as temperatures rose, because thermal stress suppresses steroid hormone production.

But testosterone and progesterone didn’t fall. They stayed elevated at 34°C, and at disease lesion sites too.

The decline in steroid precursors suggested that the coral itself was not responsible for the elevated hormone levels.

Something else within the coral and its microbial community appeared to be producing them.

That something was Endozoicomonas. Its genome encodes 17β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase, an enzyme that synthesises testosterone from androstenedione. This is a reaction normally associated with vertebrates, not bacteria.

The team confirmed it in the lab with three Endozoicomonas isolates collected from hosts as far apart as the Great Barrier Reef, Taiwan, and Abu Dhabi.

All three converted cholesterol into testosterone and progesterone. In some strains, the transformation was temperature-regulated, kicking in at 34°C.

Across a broader pangenome of 29 Endozoicomonas genomes, 66 percent carried the relevant steroid metabolism genes.

This suggests the genes are conserved across the genus, in corals around the world.

“When temperatures become high, corals lose some of their ability to produce steroid hormones that are important for regulating their immune system,” Mohamed said.

“We found that their bacterial partners can compensate by producing these hormones themselves, allowing the coral to activate an immune response that helps its cells survive the heat.”

To test that directly, the team dosed Acropora downingi nubbins with 10 nM testosterone or progesterone, then raised the temperature to 33°C – 1.5°C above the local summer maximum.

They tracked gene expression across 27 immune-related pathways.

Both hormones amplified the coral’s innate immune response. TNF receptor signalling went up, NF-κB subunits increased, and kinase cascades linked to cell survival were activated.

Progesterone had an additional effect. It reduced signals associated with inflammation while increasing activity in pathways that help keep immune responses under control.

The pattern looks less like the immune system being switched on and more like it being tuned. The transcriptional changes were largely gone after 24 hours, so the modulation was transient.

Bacterially derived steroids influencing host immune signalling aren’t entirely without precedent. Gut bacteria in mammals, including Bacteroides acidifaciens and Ruminococcus gnavus, do something similar.

But nobody had seen it in coral until now. The evolutionary conservation of the pathway across Endozoicomonas strains from different oceans and different host species suggests it isn’t accidental.

These bacteria may provide an important advantage by helping corals regulate their immune responses as ocean temperatures rise.

Coral conservation has focused almost entirely on the coral itself – heat-tolerant selective breeding, assisted gene flow, nurseries, restoration.

This study suggests the microbiome deserves more precise attention than it has received.

The right bacteria may be performing chemical work that corals struggle to carry out when ocean temperatures climb.

Scientists still need to determine whether these microbial partnerships can be strengthened or managed to improve coral survival. But the discovery reveals another layer of reef resilience, one that has been hiding inside the coral itself.

The study is published in the journal Nature Microbiology.
