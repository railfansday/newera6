The Jeep Shortcut Concept Is the Brand Incarnate

📅 03 November 2026 | Sumber: Baca Asli

We borrowed Jeep’s Shortcut Easter Safari concept to celebrate 85 years of open-top enthusiasm. It’s everything right about the seven-barred grille.

Automobiles are an undeniable part of the American way of life. And while, for many, these machines represent little more than transportation, cars and trucks have shaped the very fabric of the nation we inhabit today. No vehicle is as tied to an idealistic view of America more than the venerable Jeep, which celebrates 85 years in 2026. In order to celebrate almost nine decades of open-air enjoyment, Road & Track got to borrow a special Easter Safari concept that best represents the brand’s core tenets.

Built in 2016 for the brand’s 75th-anniversary festivities, Shortcut is quite simply one of the coolest Easter Jeep Safari projects ever to come out of Auburn Hills. Inspired by the venerable CJ-5 that launched the brand into the mainstream, this chopped-up special visually shares very little with the JK-generation Wrangler Rubicon on which it is based. The transformation is so thorough that even Jeep fans near the brand’s headquarters had a hard time placing the SUV’s origins.

The transformation sees some 14 inches of the SUV’s body removed and replaced with bespoke panels, as well as unique fascias and bumpers that lop off another foot or so from the package. Despite that substantial reduction in body size, the SUV’s wheelbase remains completely untouched to maximize capability. Everything in front of the windshield is made from carbon fiber, while the body sides themselves were hand-welded by some of Jeep’s most skilled technicians. The lack of doors makes ingress a cinch for those with some height behind them but proved to be a challenge for shorter drivers. The roof is a simple Bimini affair, supported in part by a vintage-style roll hoop in the back. Some other specific design highlights include the genuine CJ fender badges that the team welded into the JK, as well as the vintage tailgate.

Mechanically, the Shortcut isn’t that different from other JK Wranglers. Under the hood sits a Pentastar 3.6-liter V-6 mated to an automatic transmission, which remains stock aside from the relocated accessories, owing to a smaller engine bay. The suspension setup is slightly enhanced over a standard Rubicon, complete with a two-inch lift from Fox and 35-inch all-terrain rubber. Switches for the lockers and sway-bar disconnect are hidden on the steering column, but the functionality remains intact. It may not be the most exciting list of parts, but the full combination cements this SUV among the most engaging and enjoyable Jeep products I’ve sampled yet.

The joyous nature of the Shortcut and the original CJ-5 that inspired it sit in striking contrast to the situation that birthed the roots of the famous brand some 85 years ago. Long before the United States entered World War II in December 1941, the military began to make moves toward preparedness. By June 1940, the Army had tasked well over 100 automakers with submitting proposals for a quarter-ton reconnaissance vehicle aimed at closing the gap with the Wehrmacht’s ever-growing fleet. It was the likes of Ford, Willys-Overland, and Bantam together that were responsible for the production iteration of the 4x4 (Willys wouldn’t file a trademark for the Jeep name until 1943).

The Jeep would go on to serve across a number of roles in WWII, making itself an indisputable part of U.S. strategy. In fact, the chief of staff of the U.S. Army, General George Marshall, went so far as to describe the plucky 4x4 as “America’s greatest contribution to modern warfare,” which might explain why some 650,000 were pressed into service between 1941 and ’45.

Jeeps hit the open market almost as soon as the war drew to a close. The Willys CJ-2A arrived in 1945 as essentially a military truck turned farm machine. That trend continued through the CJ-2B and CJ-3A models, with limited mass-market appeal as a result. The Korean War brought a new variant of the military version known as the M38A1, which donned rounder fenders and a taller hood to accommodate the new 134-cubic-inch Hurricane F-Head four-cylinder engine from Kaiser. Civilian versions of the M38A1, known as the CJ-5, began production in 1954, kicking off a near three-decade sales run. The resulting machine not only changed the iconography of Jeep forever, but it also brought off-roaders to the mainstream in a very real way. It’s these machines that Shortcut looks back to for its design.

The core ethos of the 4x4 remained untouched with the arrival of the civilian Jeep. Early CJ-5s didn’t even come with a roof or doors as standard equipment, as these were too fussy and expensive. The former troops making up the expected buyer pool were already accustomed to the open-air experience either way. And while the CJ-5 would add various creature comforts (like hard tops) and improved powertrains over the course of its run, they were never what you’d call nice. That said, the CJ is simple to drive, easy to place on the trails, and packs enough capability to make short work of the different forms of wheeling you’ll find across this country.

This Jeep arrived as a machine that would happily take you across the Great Plains and over the Rockies. You could feel like Lewis and Clark on their westward journey for just $1475. The marketing copy basically wrote itself. The association with adventure and discovery seemingly worked, as Jeep would sell just under 605,000 CJ-5s before the run came to an end in 1983.

Like with the original CJs, driving the Shortcut is unsurprisingly colored by its lack of stature. The effectively zero-degree approach and departure angles mean that you can see the driver’s-side tires at all times. I simply had to tilt my head to check tire placement and bumper clearance, which would have proved extremely helpful if Michigan had any rocks to speak of. The unmodified wheelbase also meant I wasn’t left fighting a wayward chassis, though the shortening didn’t exactly improve the perception of on-road stability. I wasn’t exactly itching to push beyond the 45-mph posted limit. The concept clearly feels like the Jeeps of yesteryear despite all the added technology. It looks the part, too, with several passers-by asking if it was a genuine CJ-5. The grille, which was an early preview of the JL-generation model, is perhaps the biggest giveaway to the well informed.

The truck is set up to be a true off-road weapon, which probably explains why it’s been back to Moab nearly every year since 2016. In fact, the Shortcut gets more regular use on the trails than any other modern Jeep concept. It is reportedly used as the training vehicle for members of the Jeep team on their first Easter Safari rides, owing to a combination of its ease of use and the fundamental “Jeepness” that surrounds the experience. If you’ve been out to the Easter Jeep Safari event, there’s a great chance you’ve seen it leading the charge. It still wears the battle scars to prove it.

A lot has changed in the 10 years since the Shortcut first appeared. The public’s perception of America and our place in the world has shifted. Jeep itself is now part of a massive multinational conglomerate headquartered in the Netherlands. The country we live in today is unrecognizable in many ways from the one that birthed the CJ-5, for better and for worse. And yet, the fundamental basis of the Jeep brand remains steadfast. Their best machines are able to safely take you places you otherwise would not go. They make you feel confident and skilled. And without a roof or any doors to get in the way, they connect you to the act of driving (and the brand’s roots) in a way that few other machines truly can.

All of that is why the Wrangler continues to create genuine enthusiasts at a rate that your average off-roader will never rival. The automotive landscape is going to look a helluva lot different 85 years from now, but as long as there is an American auto industry, I’m willing to bet an open-top Jeep is going to be a part of that equation.
