Could Social Security turmoil influence senior homeowners and reverse mortgage demand?

📅 03 November 2026 | Sumber: Baca Asli

A 13% surge in early Social Security retirement claims in 2024–2025, driven by political and administrative turmoil at the agency, temporarily reversed a long-term trend of Americans claiming later. Early filing locks in permanently lower benefits, cutting annual income by thousands of dollars for some retirees. Although claim volumes have since normalized, ongoing uncertainty about Social Security’s 2032 funding cliff is pushing some near-retirees to claim sooner.  AI Summary

A sharp but temporary spike in early Social Security claims in 2024 and 2025 underscores a potential trend for the housing market — a greater number of older Americans who are locking in permanently lower retirement income in response to political and administrative uncertainty.

Over the weekend, The New York Times followed up on a report shared last year by HousingWire‘s Reverse Mortgage Daily (RMD). The initial report from the Times found that from October 2024 to April 2025, retirement claims jumped by 267,000, or 13%, compared with the prior year.

Historically, year-over-year fluctuations have been closer to 2% to 3%. Analysts at the Urban Institute and the Center for Retirement Research at Boston College tied the spike to highly publicized turmoil at the Social Security Administration — including staff cuts, office-closure threats and political rhetoric questioning the program’s solvency.

Social Security claims made before the full retirement age (which is now 67 for those born in 1960 or later) permanently reduces monthly benefits. This week, the Times interviewed two claimants — one who gave up about $5,000 a year by filing at 64 and another who sacrificed about $300 a month by filing more than a year early.

These kinds of benefit cuts compound over time and can directly affect housing affordability, especially for owners or renters whose largest fixed expense is their mortgage or rent payment.

As it relates to housing professionals, including reverse mortgage professionals, lower guaranteed income in retirement can translate into a higher likelihood of mortgage payment stress or default among senior homeowners. It can also propel demand for downsizing, shared housing options or migration to lower-cost markets as retirees adjust to tighter budgets.

Additionally, it can mean increased interest for traditional reverse mortgages and similar home equity release products to backfill income gaps. Borrowers in their late 50s and early 60s may be ideally positioned for these products since Social Security filing decisions are imminent, and because seniors are denied for credit at higher rates than the general population.

From October 2025 to April 2026, new claims fell by 235,000, or 10%, essentially offsetting the prior-year surge. But the reporting shows how headlines about Social Security’s finances can directly change claimants’ behavior.

A Boston College experiment cited by the Times this week found that more alarmist headlines led people to say they would claim about a year earlier than those who saw neutral language — regardless of any changes to the underlying facts.

Without congressional action, Social Security’s retirement trust fund is projected to be depleted by late 2032, triggering an automatic benefit cut of up to 22% under current law. The ongoing uncertainty has made Americans in retirement and near retirement “anxious and unhappy,” according to a source who spoke to the Times. This can impact retirement timing, where they choose to live and how much home they can afford.

Multiple policy ideas are circulating on the Social Security front — including bipartisan proposals to lift or eliminate the current $184,500 cap on wages subject to payroll tax, which could inject about $3 trillion into the system over 10 years. But for now, reverse mortgage companies and other housing stakeholders should pay close attention to how Social Security claiming decisions interact with mortgage terms, home equity levels and long-term retirement planning.

In this week’s Monday Morning Q&A, Larissa Morrell of Longbridge Financial told RMD that the demographics of the typical borrower have shifted during her 20-plus years in the industry.

“Historically, a reverse mortgage was really something for people of the older retiree demographic — late 70s, 80s, 90s. Now we do not have that same demographic. Now we’re looking at people of a younger generation. They’re still working. They might be using a reverse mortgage to pay off credit card debt or cover increasing expenses,” Morrell said.

Federally insured Home Equity Conversion Mortgages and proprietary reverse mortgages could allow a borrower to delay Social Security claims by generating additional cash flow or eliminating monthly mortgage payments.

The Social Security Administration notes that people who begin receiving benefits at the full retirement age of 67 get 100% of the monthly benefit. But that percentage increases for every month payments are delayed and can reach up to 124% if a recipients waits until age 70 to make a claim.

But the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau has also previously reported that the costs of a reverse mortgage generally exceed the additional increase in benefits that a senior would get by delaying claims.
