A common substance in everyday foods is linked to better memory

📅 03 November 2026 | Sumber: Baca Asli

Older adults in the study who ate more lecithin, found in eggs and soybeans, tended to score better on thinking tests.

Eggs for breakfast, beans with lunch, meat at dinner: each can provide lecithin, a mixture of fatty substances found in many foods.

Lecithin has a reputation as a memory aid, although the evidence is thin.

In a Chinese study, older adults with more lecithin in their diets had slightly better memory and thinking scores.

They also had lower odds of mild cognitive impairment: thinking problems that are worse than normal aging, but haven’t reached dementia.

The researchers assessed everyone just once. That gave them a snapshot of diet and memory, but no way to tell whether lecithin caused the difference.

Two hospital researchers in Beijing, Xiaoying Li and Chengyan Qi, are the first authors. Both hospitals are affiliated with Capital Medical University, where coauthor Yuandi Xi, a public health professor, studies nutrition, memory and muscle loss in older adults.

Cells use lecithin in the thin membranes that surround them. When we digest lecithin, we also get choline, which the body uses to make a chemical messenger that carries signals between nerve cells.

So there is a reason to look at lecithin and memory together.

A Finnish study involving 2,497 middle-aged men linked higher dietary phosphatidylcholine intake with a lower risk of dementia.

But lecithin has a less promising record as a treatment for dementia. A review of randomized trials, published in 2003, found that the evidence didn’t support using it that way.

Li and Qi’s team wanted to know about the years before dementia. Was getting more lecithin through ordinary meals linked with better memory and thinking?

The work involved 1,917 people in three Chinese cities: Beijing, Jincheng and Linyi.

The team recruited them between 2020 and 2023 from census or village records; every participant was 60 or older and had lived locally for at least a year.

In face-to-face interviews, they talked about their health and the foods they ate. A questionnaire recorded how often they ate different foods.

Using those answers and China’s food composition database, the researchers estimated each person’s daily lecithin intake.

About 88 milligrams marked the midpoint, so the main comparison was between people who ate more and those who ate less.

The team used a 30-point test called the Montreal Cognitive Assessment to screen for memory and thinking problems. A low score meant different things depending on a person’s education.

A score of 13 or below flagged possible trouble for someone who could not read. For people with seven or more years of schooling, the cutoff was 24 or below.

Neurologists examined everyone flagged by the screening. They used criteria from the U.S. National Institute on Aging and the Alzheimer’s Association to decide who had mild cognitive impairment.

They diagnosed 1,023 of the 1,917 participants, just over half the group.

The two intake groups were separated by about half a point, with the advantage going to those above 88 milligrams a day.

That calculation took account of age, sex, schooling, calories, blood fats, body composition and seven chronic diseases, including diabetes and stroke.

Half a point was a modest difference on this test. The typical score was 23 for people without mild cognitive impairment and 19 for those with it.

The higher-lecithin group had about 20% lower odds of a diagnosis. Dividing participants into thirds by intake gave a similar result: the top third had about 27% lower odds than the bottom third.

The team described the work as epidemiological evidence, meaning it revealed a health pattern across a group of people.

“This study provides important epidemiological evidence that ensuring adequate lecithin intake through a balanced diet could be a feasible approach to support cognitive health in the elderly,” the research team wrote.

The authors pointed to China’s daily reference intake for choline: 450 milligrams for men and 380 for women. These are choline targets, so they should not be confused with the lecithin amounts measured here.

According to the authors, a balanced diet with eggs, legumes and lean meats can readily meet those choline targets.

Earlier studies, they noted, have also linked eating patterns such as the Mediterranean diet with better thinking later in life.

The clearest link appeared among people with the least education. Of those taking part, 1,025 had gone no further than primary school, including 387 who could not read.

Within this group, people eating more lecithin scored nearly a point higher. For the other 892, who had reached junior high school or beyond, there was barely any gap.

The pattern didn’t vary reliably with age, sex or the chronic diseases the team checked. Education was different, and the authors think cognitive reserve could help explain why.

Schooling and mental effort may give the brain more ways to cope with damage.

Someone with less of that reserve might be more sensitive to dietary differences, although the study cannot establish whether changing their meals would help.

Red blood cell membranes offered another way to compare the groups, since they can reflect fats eaten over time.

For this part, the team randomly chose 15 people with mild cognitive impairment and 15 without it, then examined blood samples.

There was less lecithin in the cell membranes of people with impairment. And what was there contained a different mix: less of it carried polyunsaturated fatty acids, while more carried saturated fatty acids.

One member of the polyunsaturated family is DHA, a fat found in high amounts in nerve cells.

A form of lecithin carrying linoleic acid, another polyunsaturated fat, was one of six membrane fats that best distinguished the groups.

This was a small comparison, with only 30 people in all. The analysis also could not single out lecithin carrying DHA.

One thing this study cannot settle is the direction of the link. Diet and thinking were measured together, so it’s possible that memory problems had already changed people’s eating habits.

The team had no information on household income or household size, which can influence both diet and brain health. Participants also had to remember what they ate, leaving room for errors.

All participants came from three cities in China, and doctors made diagnoses without a full set of memory tests.

Studies elsewhere might find a different pattern.

The authors suggested that asking about lecithin intake could eventually become an inexpensive first step in community memory screening.

A model that combined intake with age, sex, schooling, calories and several health measures told the two groups apart reasonably well.

For now, the study cannot tell us whether eating more lecithin slows memory decline. To find out, the authors called for research that follows older adults for years, tracking their meals and their thinking.

The study is published in the journal Chinese Medical Journal.
