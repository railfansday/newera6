Your clothes could soon work like a smartphone touchscreen

📅 03 November 2026 | Sumber: Baca Asli

Scientists have developed the world's first wearable fabric touchscreen, creating washable clothing that displays images, plays videos, and responds to touch.

Phone and laptop screens are built on flat, stiff panels. Clothing is made of soft threads. Until now, those threads couldn’t form a full-color screen that responds to touch.

In Singapore, engineers have built that kind of screen into the forearm of a sleeve, and it kept working after 50 trips through a washing machine.

There’s no rigid screen anywhere in the sleeve.

Tural Khudiyev, an assistant professor in the Department of Materials Science and Engineering at the National University of Singapore (NUS), led the work. His team at the university’s College of Design and Engineering published it on October 21, 2026.

The menus and buttons people tap on a phone make up what engineers call a graphical user interface, or GUI.

Khudiyev’s team built one from electronic fibers, creating a working touchscreen on the forearm of a sleeve.

“What made this possible was our ability to construct the GUI system from electronic building blocks in fiber form – similar to traditional yarn, but with the sophisticated electronic functionality built directly into the fibers,” Khudiyev said.

Fabric that lights up isn’t new. A 2021 paper in the journal Nature described large display textiles, while another study in 2025 described a single-fiber computer.

Khudiyev’s team cites both. Earlier approaches, the researchers wrote, don’t readily give each pixel its own digital address or move information fast enough for touchscreen menus.

One obstacle was a fiber’s round, thin shape.

“A fiber has a one-dimensional, axially symmetric geometry, which makes it difficult to build complex circuitry using approaches developed for conventional two-dimensional electronics,” Khudiyev added.

“We overcame this limitation through a new interconnection architecture, which enables thousands of microelectronic devices to be integrated and interconnected along a fiber.”

The team started with tiny wires wrapped in insulation. A laser exposed the metal only at the spots where each microchip needed a connection.

The engineers controlled where molten solder, a metal alloy used to join electronic parts, could spread, allowing it to bond only at the exposed spots.

With that method, the team joined more than 5,000 chips through over 20,000 electrical connections. Next, the engineers built the strip of LEDs into a layered fiber.

They used thermal drawing, in which heated material is pulled into a long, thin strand. The finished thread is about 0.04 inch (1 millimeter) thick and about 33 feet (10 meters) long.

For the prototype, the team threaded 54 of these fibers into the forearm of a sleeve. Each fiber carries 96 LEDs, so the screen is 96 pixels wide and 54 pixels tall.

Underneath the LEDs, a separate layer senses touch. Pressing the fiber completes a circuit, and the signal shows where the finger landed, so the nearest pixel lights up.

The whole screen measures about 6.6 by 4.1 inches (168 by 105 millimeters), a little larger than a postcard. It refreshes 30 times a second and supports 8-bit color.

A full-HD TV picture has 400 times as many pixels, so the sleeve shows far less detail.

Each fiber has its own control channel. If one line fails, the fibers next to it keep working normally, and the screen can register several touches at once.

Other fibers, built the same way, carry a microphone, a speaker and sensors that track movement, find magnetic north, measure temperature and gauge how bright the surroundings are.

Together, the fibers handle jobs that early smartphones could do.

The sleeve runs a calculator and a photo gallery, and it plays video at 30 frames per second, or 25 when the sound is synced. It works as a compass, responds to voice commands and receives images over Bluetooth.

The sleeve can rotate the screen on its own. It adjusts its brightness, too.

When the temperature fiber is placed under the arm, the sleeve reads the wearer’s body temperature and shows it on the display.

The team ran the sleeve through 50 detergent washes in a typical washing machine. They also bent it 1,000 times around a curve just 0.2 inch (5 millimeters) in radius.

After both tests, the light it gave off and its response to touch were nearly unchanged.

The display lets air through about as well as cotton, polyester and denim do. It also stays within established safety limits for contact with skin.

Smaller chips and thinner fibers also fit the team’s method, and they could lead to sharper, larger screens. Thinner fibers could be knitted, woven and embroidered on the standard machines factories already use.

Smartwatches and other wearable technology already track some body signals from the wrist.

“Healthcare is one particularly promising application,” Khudiyev said.

“A garment could continuously collect physiological information through fiber-based sensors and allow the wearer to access and interact with that information directly through their clothing.”

“Combined with artificial intelligence (AI), this could enable personalized health monitoring, feedback and assistance,” Khudiyev added.

“Just as smartphones enabled applications that were difficult to imagine when they first appeared, garment GUIs could ultimately enable entirely new applications that do not yet exist.”

Tracking more health signs and giving touch-based feedback are uses the team describes for the future, not results from the prototype.

Future versions could offer sharper displays, more built-in sensors and AI-powered features that expand what clothing can do.

The full study was published in the journal Nature.
