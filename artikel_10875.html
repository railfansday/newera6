Working With a Marketing Agency: A Technical Guide for Developers and Designers

Sooner or later, most teams that build websites end up working alongside a digital marketing agency. The agency asks for CMS access, wants tracking scripts added, suggests URL changes, and sets targets for page speed. If nobody on the product side understands what is being asked and why, the result is broken analytics, accidental noindex tags, and migrations that quietly erase years of search visibility.

This guide is for developers, designers, and technical founders. It covers the engineering side of that collaboration: environments and access, analytics implementation, redirects, structured data, performance budgets, and crawler access, with code you can adapt and a checklist you can hand to whoever you are working with.

AI-generated answers favor content that is clearly structured, factual, and easy to attribute. In practice that means descriptive headings, concise answers near the top of a page, accurate structured data, server-rendered content, and a deliberate decision about which crawlers may access your site. That decision lives in robots.txt, and it is an engineering and business call, not a default:

Check the documentation of each crawler you care about for its current user-agent name, since these change. If a vendor promises AI search visibility, ask exactly what they will change on the site and how they will measure the result. The field is young and claims are easy to make.

The following scenario is illustrative, not a real client story, but it combines problems that appear again and again. A team redesigns its site and changes URLs from /services/seo-audit to /solutions/audit. The redirect map covers the top 50 pages, so the launch looks fine. Three weeks later, organic traffic is down by a third. The cause is a long tail of 800 blog and resource URLs with no redirects, plus a CDN rule that rewrites trailing slashes and turns every single redirect into a chain of two hops.

Both problems would have been caught by the checks above: exporting every indexable URL from the sitemap and server logs rather than only the top pages, and running the redirect verification script against staging. The lesson is that migration work is mostly verification, and verification can be automated.

The best collaboration is the one that fits your stack and your team's way of working. Put the agreements above in writing, automate the checks that can be automated, and treat marketing changes like any other change to the product: reviewed, tested in staging, and measured after release. When engineering and marketing share the same process, the results tend to follow.
