Liquidation isn't triggered by a price — it's triggered by running out of margin

📅 03 November 2026 | Sumber: Baca Asli

Posted on Nov 3 • Originally published at community.deeprade.com

Last week roughly $1.6 billion in crypto positions was liquidated in a single day after an exchange reported a theft. Almost none of those traders were wrong about the direction. They were wrong about a rule: how much margin a position must keep to stay open.

Here is the mechanism, in the order it actually fires.

Most venues compute liquidation against a mark price — an index built from several spot exchanges — rather than the last trade on their own book. The reason is protective: a single wick on one exchange shouldn't vaporise positions across the market.

If you build anything that reads candles and shows a liquidation line, decide explicitly which price you are using and label it. Mixing them is the single most common source of wrong backtests.

Those rates are per-exchange and per size tier, and higher leverage usually carries a higher maintenance rate. Read the venue's own rules page before you hard-code anything — a bot that assumes 0.5% everywhere will be confidently wrong.

A liquidation order is a market order. It takes liquidity rather than providing it. The first wave eats the best bids, the mark price moves another tick, more positions cross their threshold, and those orders hit the book too. Nothing new needs to happen for the waterfall to continue — the mechanism is self-feeding while depth is thin.

For software this matters twice: your simulator must model slippage against a shrinking book, and your risk layer should treat "distance to liquidation" as a non-linear quantity, not a linear buffer.

The difference isn't about which earns more. It's about which one survives a wick.
