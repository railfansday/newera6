Mountains are eroding fastest where you least expect it

📅 03 November 2026 | Sumber: Baca Asli

Scientists have discovered why some gentle mountain landscapes erode faster than steep rocky slopes, revealing hidden effects on Earth's carbon cycle.

Every mountain range is, in geological terms, falling apart. Rivers cut downward, slopes shed material, rock weathers into soil and sediment and eventually finds its way to the sea.

Geologists have been measuring this process – denudation – for decades, using a clever trick involving cosmic rays.

When high-energy particles from exploded stars hit quartz grains at the surface, they create a rare isotope, beryllium-10, at a known rate.

The longer a grain sits exposed, the more ¹⁰Be it accumulates. Measure it, and you get an erosion rate.

The problem is that this has always worked better on some rocks than others. In granite, sandstone, and other coarse-grained rocks, quartz grains are large and easy to isolate.

In shale – one of the most common sedimentary rocks on earth, soft, fine-grained, and widespread across mountain landscapes – the quartz is too small and dispersed to extract cleanly using standard techniques.

As a result, shale has often been left out of erosion measurements, creating a significant gap in our understanding of how mountain landscapes change.

A team led by Eyal Marder at the University of Massachusetts Amherst has now fixed that. Their study describes a new method for extracting ¹⁰Be from fine-grained sedimentary rocks.

The researchers applied this method to the East River watershed in the Elk and West Elk mountains of Colorado.

This is a 465-square-kilometer catchment where resistant igneous rock and Cretaceous Mancos Shale sit side by side, subject to the same climate and the same tectonic forces.

“Shale’s erodibility is three times that of the harder rocks in the East River Watershed,” Marder said.

The shale catchments in the study erode at rates ranging from 0.05 to 0.38 mm per year, with a median of 0.11 mm per year. The intrusive igneous catchments erode at 0.03 to 0.07 mm per year.

That gap, on its own, is not surprising: geologists have long assumed weaker rock erodes faster. What the measurements reveal is the consequence of that contrast playing out over millions of years.

When weak and strong rocks sit next to each other in a landscape, they don’t erode in sync. The shale comes down faster while maintaining comparatively gentle, subdued topography.

The harder rock develops steeper slopes and higher relief as it adjusts more slowly. The result is that the fastest erosion in a mountain range is happening in the rounded, lower-relief shale terrain.

“When we look at landscapes, we are only seeing a snapshot in time,” Marder said. And that snapshot is misleading.

A steep cliff face of hard granite looks like where the action is. The quiet shale valley is where the ground is actually disappearing.

Shale and igneous rock don’t just erode at different rates. They also have opposite effects on atmospheric CO2.

Intrusive rocks like granite and monzonite undergo silicate weathering, a process that draws CO2 out of the atmosphere and locks it into dissolved carbonate.

Shale, by contrast, can contain pyrite and organic carbon, whose weathering can contribute to CO2 release.

In the East River watershed, chemical weathering accounts for roughly 10 to 70 percent of the total denudation rate.

Because the shale is eroding three times faster than the igneous rock, it is contributing disproportionately to CO2 release.

The landscape evolution modelling in the study suggests this imbalance persists for millions of years.

The models suggest that shale continues to erode faster than harder rock over extended periods, sustaining the difference in their contributions to the carbon cycle.

The cumulative CO2 implications are large.

“Scale our work up from the Elk and West Elk mountains to all the mountainous regions of the world, and getting erosion rates and their equivalent impact on CO2 release right has a marked impact on what we know about biogeochemical global cycles and climate change,” Marder explained.

Model simulations predict that the watershed could release approximately 85 teragrams of CO2 over 2.6 million years of continuous landscape adjustment.

That could potentially rise to 135 Tg if repeated glacial topographic resetting drives repeated transience cycles.

These are first-order estimates, and they exclude CO2 from oxidative weathering of fossil organic carbon in shale, which would push the numbers higher still.

The deeper lesson of the study is about what topography alone cannot tell you. Slope, relief, and channel steepness are the standard proxies geologists use to assess erosion rates and landscape equilibrium.

The steeper the terrain, the assumption goes, the faster it’s eroding. That assumption holds when the rock type is uniform.

Where it isn’t, the fastest erosion is hidden in the gentlest terrain. Models that ignore lithological contrasts will systematically misread both the erosion and the carbon flux.

The new ¹⁰Be technique for fine-grained sediments should allow researchers to fill in those gaps across other mixed-lithology mountain belts – the Appalachians, the Alps, the Himalayas.

There, shale and weak sedimentary sequences are common and their contribution to erosion and chemical weathering has been poorly constrained.

Getting that accounting right matters for anyone trying to understand how mountains regulate Earth’s carbon cycle over geological time.

The study is published in the journal Science Advances.
