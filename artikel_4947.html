The Hidden Cost of Chronic Stress (and 6 Ways to Fight Back)

Mortgage payments. Looming layoffs. An upcoming medical procedure. Growing tensions at home.

One worry after another races through your mind on the drive home from work. Just then, your phone rings. A voicemail from your boss: “I need to see you first thing tomorrow in my office.”

By the time you get home, you have a pretty bad headache. Before your partner says anything, you mumble, “I'm really stressed out.”

Generally speaking, stress is a psychological and physiological response to demands and perceived threats that require coping or adaptation.

This “alarm” (i.e., the stress response) can be useful, of course. It helped our ancestors navigate threats to survival: finding food, escaping predators, protecting what they had, and dealing with conflicts in their social groups.

The issue is that this system, which evolved to protect us from threats to survival, is easily and repeatedly activated by modern pressures. It is activated by situations that are perceived as uncertain, uncontrollable, or threatening to things we value (e.g., our finances, health, relationships, etc.): Unpaid bills. Traffic jams. Unanswered emails. Ring. Ring. Ring.

There are two interacting systems in the stress response: the sympathetic-adrenal-medullary (SAM) system and the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis. Yes, that’s a mouthful.

SAM is the body’s fast stress pathway. Only seconds after getting a concerning call from your boss, for example, SAM triggers the release of adrenaline and noradrenaline, which narrows attention, increases heart rate and breathing, directs blood flow toward large muscles, and prepares the body to fight or flee.

The HPA axis, which operates more slowly, controls the release of cortisol, known as the “stress hormone.” Cortisol helps make more energy (i.e., glucose) available for the body to use and coordinates many other physiological changes. These effects help the body cope with prolonged challenges.

HPA and SAM activity are part of allostasis, which is the process of keeping the body functioning by continuously adjusting its internal state to meet changing demands.

Over time, however, repeatedly activating these adaptive systems can produce allostatic load, which is the cumulative wear and tear that chronic stress places on the body.

Thankfully, our bodies also have a way to turn the stress response off. Once a threat has passed, a branch of the autonomic nervous system called the parasympathetic nervous system helps restore the balance. This is the body’s “rest and digest” system. It slows the heart, regulates breathing, and allows your body to shift its focus back to maintenance and recovery.

Typically, the body moves between activation and recovery. The stress response switches on when a threat is detected and then off once the threat has passed. When a stressor is brief and has a clear endpoint, the body can return toward its baseline relatively quickly. But not all stressors are like that.

For many people in modern societies, daily stressors are less about immediate physical threats and more about psychological and social pressures; that is, chronic, low-grade stressors that are difficult to escape:

We go to bed worrying about health, family, finances, and a million other things. Then wake up... and pick up where we left off.

So it appears we have a survival system that is mismatched to important aspects of our environment. Though it protects us in the short term, when this system is activated too frequently or for too long, it can have serious consequences. For instance, it can negatively affect immune function, tissue repair, digestion, reproduction, and many other functions. Over time, these changes can contribute to conditions such as back pain, obesity, diabetes, and heart disease. Chronic stress can affect mental health too, contributing to anxiety, depression, insomnia, relationship difficulties, and alcohol/drug misuse.

We cannot eliminate many of the modern stressors—bills, deadlines, and traffic are not going anywhere. But what we can do is give our bodies more opportunities to restore physiological balance, and prevent the negative effects of chronic stress.

1. One of the best-studied ways to regulate the stress response is exercise. Whether you enjoy dancing, jogging, swimming, or skipping rope, you may find physical activity a wonderful way to not only reduce stress but also increase a sense of self-efficacy and mastery.

2. Nutrition matters too. Eating a nutritious, plant-rich diet may support our ability to cope with stress, although exactly how healthy eating affects the stress response remains an active area of research.

3. Recovery isn't only physical; it's social too. When we feel socially supported, we are less likely to perceive stressful situations as threatening.

4. Walking in nature is another way we can take a break from the sensory overload of our technology-dependent lives. Research has shown that spending time in nature reduces stress and promotes positive feelings.

5. Also consider mind-body practices such as mindfulness meditation, yoga, and tai chi. These can be great stress-relievers. A systematic review found meditation was associated with not only reductions in cortisol but also in blood pressure and C-reactive protein (an inflammation marker).

6. Finally, prioritize sleep. Yes, easier said than done, I know, but it's worth making the effort. Sleep deprivation can heighten feelings of stress and anxiety, making everyday challenges more difficult to manage.

The goal of these strategies isn't to eliminate stress completely, which is impossible anyway and not helpful either. After all, we need the stress response to protect us from genuine threats. The challenge is giving that system enough opportunities to deactivate so we can rest and recover.

In our modern world, the alarm may never stop ringing, but with these strategies, we can help our bodies and minds return to baseline again. And again. And again.
