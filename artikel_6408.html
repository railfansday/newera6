Satellites reveal hidden flooding along the North Carolina coast

📅 03 November 2026 | Sumber: Baca Asli

Scientists combined satellite data to create detailed daily flood maps of North Carolina wetlands, revealing hidden flooding that could influence methane emissions.

Flooding along the North Carolina coast doesn’t always look dramatic. There may be no raging rivers or streets swallowed by water.

Sometimes, the difference between dry land and a flooded landscape is just a few inches.

The Albemarle-Pamlico Peninsula just sits there, nearly flat, less than a meter above sea level across almost half its surface, buffered from the Atlantic by a thin chain of barrier islands.

The soils are organic muck. The forests are part hardwood swamp, part moss.

More than 70 percent of the land spends some portion of the year underwater – and has done so long before anyone started worrying about sea-level rise.

It is also, for exactly these reasons, one of the harder places on earth to watch from space. A team at North Carolina State University spent five years trying.

They used a method that fuses radar and optical satellite data into daily flood probability maps at six-meter resolution. That’s fine-grained enough to catch the small, short-lived inundation events that standard products miss entirely.

Wetlands are the planet’s largest natural methane source.

How much methane a wetland releases depends partly on how long and how deeply it floods.

Waterlogged soils contain less oxygen, creating conditions that allow methane-producing microbes to thrive. Brief and small floods matter too.

And yet current global estimates of inundated area carry enough uncertainty to account for roughly a third of what we don’t understand about atmospheric methane.

Most of that uncertainty comes from wetlands smaller than 1,000 square meters. These wetlands are flooding and draining faster than satellites with 30-meter pixels and 16-day revisit cycles can follow.

The study compared three sensors before combining them. Sentinel-2, the European Space Agency’s optical satellite, achieved 93.9 percent accuracy classifying dry land, inundated vegetation, and open water.

PlanetScope, the commercial CubeSat constellation operated by Planet Labs, reached 88.4 percent and resolved fine-scale open-water features particularly well.

Sentinel-1, which uses radar to observe Earth’s surface, achieved 84.6 percent accuracy. Unlike optical satellites, it can collect observations through clouds.

These limitations make it difficult to monitor flooding consistently throughout the year. Persistent cloud cover can block optical satellite observations for weeks at a time.

PlanetScope lacks the shortwave infrared band that most reliably separates water from waterlogged vegetation. Stack those limitations and a single-sensor flood record has systematic gaps.

The team chose to classify each sensor independently and then combine the results, rather than training a joint model from scratch.

The logic was practical: with limited training data, a combined model risks learning the quirks of the training set rather than the actual landscape.

Keeping the sensors separate preserves what each does best – Sentinel-2’s infrared sensitivity, Sentinel-1’s backscatter ratios, and PlanetScope’s spatial density. Using them together handles the disagreements.

To combine the observations, the researchers used a statistical technique called fixed-rank kriging. The method analyzes how flooding changes across both space and time.

Instead of simply averaging the satellite measurements, it accounts for differences in their reliability. This allows scientists to estimate where flooding is likely to occur, even when direct observations are missing.

The result is a five-year record of daily flood probability maps at six-meter resolution, complete with estimates of uncertainty.

The maps were validated against water-table measurements from two AmeriFlux flux towers, a USGS stream gauge, and lake-elevation records. They caught short flood pulses that no individual sensor detected.

The peninsula includes the Alligator River National Wildlife Refuge and one of the East Coast’s largest pocosin complexes.

It’s threaded with drainage ditches and canals dug over centuries for agriculture and forestry, now interacting in unpredictable ways with rising groundwater and Atlantic storm surge.

The microtopography alone makes inundation complex. Tree bases sit on hummocks above the waterline while the surrounding ground stays permanently saturated. No single satellite pass, on any given day, can fully capture that.

That complexity is increasingly normal for low-lying coastal wetlands globally. The framework here is modular and sensor-agnostic.

New data streams can be added as they become available. They might include the forthcoming L-band radar satellites.

These should be able to detect flooding beneath the tree canopy that current instruments can’t reliably penetrate.

The immediate use is methane and carbon modeling.

Inundation probability can be fed directly into biogeochemical models rather than handing them a single deterministic map and pretending the uncertainty doesn’t exist.

The longer ambition is a continuous, high-resolution record of the brief floods that rarely make headlines but matter enormously. They drive greenhouse-gas exchange, support migratory wildlife, and recharge aquifers.

They also signal early-stage hydrological change – the kind that sea-level rise is forcing across the coastal zone faster than most monitoring systems can follow.

The study is published in the Journal of Remote Sensing.
