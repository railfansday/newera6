A switch made of folded DNA doesn’t flip by itself, even though heat keeps shaking it

📅 03 November 2026 | Sumber: Baca Asli

A switch folded from DNA holds its position while heat shakes it, flips on command, and in the best cases kept working through more than 200,000 electric pulses.

A light switch stays wherever you leave it, but if you shrink it to the size of a molecule, that no longer holds because heat shakes every part of it.

A team in Germany has folded DNA into a switch that holds its position anyway and flips on command, and it kept working through more than 200,000 electric pulses.

DNA origami folds DNA strands into a precise shape. Researchers at the Technical University of Munich tested the switch in the lab, with copies stuck to a glass slide, not inside a cell or body.

“With our design, we were able to show that a DNA-based switch can not only be controlled quickly and precisely, but is also exceptionally long-lived,” said Friedrich Simmel, a physics professor at the TUM School of Natural Sciences and a co-author of the study.

DNA origami takes its name from paper folding, a craft that also shapes some plant-inspired robots. At the University of Sydney Nano Institute, researchers built more than 50 nanoscale objects from DNA blocks, including a tiny dinosaur. They said the approach could lead to nanorobots that deliver drugs.

But a shape can’t do a job. A machine needs parts that move on command, and at this size, heat makes that hard. Other labs build tiny machines from human cells or plant-based gels instead.

Earlier DNA switches all had a flaw. Some needed a constant push to stay in place. Others flipped slowly or wore out fast. Many used DNA “latches” that came apart when heat jostled them.

The Munich researchers borrowed an idea from large-scale engineering, the snap-through mechanism, which rests in either of two positions and moves only when pushed hard enough. Their switch has two stiff DNA arms joined by a hinge.

The arms sit on a square base plate about two millionths of an inch across (55 nanometers), roughly a thousand times narrower than a human hair.

The arms have two resting positions. Moving from one to the other means passing each other, and where they overlap, a barrier that heat alone rarely gets past forms. In one test, the researchers watched 70 switches under a microscope for an hour. Not one flipped by itself.

The same barrier that keeps the switch still means it takes a real push to move it.

DNA carries a negative charge, so an electric field pushes on it. To use that, the researchers attached a lever to one arm; the DNA rod is more than seven times longer than the base plate is wide. When a pulse hits, the lever swings and pushes the arms past the barrier.

A flip takes milliseconds. Afterward, the switch stays put with no power at all.

One caveat first. The switches lay on the glass at random angles, and the field flips them best at one angle. So the researchers tracked only switches that flipped in a first test, and the numbers below describe switches that lined up well with the field.

In one run, a single switch got 500 pulses of 10 milliseconds each over 50 seconds and flipped on 447 of them. An hour later, it got 500 more and flipped 434 of them.

A second test used a switch as a gate. It hid and exposed a spot where other DNA strands attach, and flipping it changed how fast those strands attached. So an electric field could regulate how fast that reaction ran.

Fluorescent dyes were the first way to track each switch, with a cluster on the lever tip. Dyes fade under light, so that works for minutes, not days, but gold doesn’t fade. The researchers attached a gold rod, about as long as the base plate is wide, to the lever.

Under a microscope with a filter that favors light of one orientation, the rod looks bright when it lines up with the filter and nearly dark when it turns sideways. Each flip changed its brightness, letting the researchers track one switch for days.

Switches didn’t wear out evenly. One dropped out after 6,500 pulses. The best took 206,800 pulses over more than five and a half hours and flipped on 195,000 of them, about 94%, before its success rate began to fall.

That’s more than 30 times as many pulses as the early dropout. Some devices still flipped after several days of repeated use.

The researchers tested single switches on a glass slide. The authors envision arrays, with many switches working together, but they remain untested. The data behind the paper sit in a public Zenodo archive.

The authors also suggested the switch could serve as nanoscale memory, since it rests in one of two positions.

Simmel, first author Florian Rothfischer and co-author Enzo Kopperger have filed a patent application on the technology.

Why one switch outlasts another is still unsettled, though the setup gives the group a way to study how switches wear out and fail.

More development is needed before any of this works outside a laboratory. The authors want to build arrays of switches that work together.

The full study was published in the journal Science Robotics.
