Southwest Denies A 787 Order. Remember Its Bag Promise?

📅 03 November 2026 | Sumber: Baca Asli

On its October 22nd, 2026 earnings call, CEO, Bob Jordan, shot down reports of a widebody order as having “no credibility.” The reports came from a Reuters exclusive two weeks earlier, citing three unnamed sources who said Southwest was looking at Boeing 787s for a long-haul push, possibly leased or second-hand, with a launch window of roughly three years. At the time, Southwest’s only comment was that it had no news forthcoming on its fleet plans. We have heard this before.

His reasoning was mostly that it would require significant changes in the business model. Operating aircraft with more than 175 seats would send the airline back to its pilots, and the pilot contract likely opens next year. Another 737 variant would be “significantly easier” than a widebody. Long-haul flying would be upside “if we decide to pursue that.” My eyerolls are likely audible at this point.

I know how this goes because I have watched the free bag story (and every other unique characteristic that Southwest would “never” change) play out. In July 2024, Jordan told investors that bags flying free was the number one reason customers choose Southwest, behind only fare and schedule. On September 17, 2024, a Southwest spokesperson told The Points Guy there was “no work currently underway” to change the policy, and Jordan, pressed on the question, would only say never say never, as CBS reported.

Six months later, on March 11, 2025, Southwest ended the policy. The airline set fees of $35 for the first checked bag and $45 for the second on flights booked on or after May 28, 2025. Jordan’s explanation was that bags flying free was not part of the airline’s DNA, and that open seating was not either. What he should have said was that it wasn’t part of Elliott Management’s DNA and they were running the show – at least while they were still heavy investors. Analysts warned at the time that dropping the policy could damage the brand and turn Southwest into just another airline. It most certainly did that, but as reported in recent weeks, it has, in fact, helped the stock price and financials significantly.

But there is no demonstrable difference between Southwest and any of the top 5 US carriers except one, no long haul international service.

The bags went first, and assigned seating arrived on January 27, 2026, with eight boarding groups, four fare tiers and extra-legroom rows on every flight. Basic fares board last. Open seating, which was the other half of the cattle-call charm, was gone. This has all been covered over the last 18 months but it’s important preamble when considering the next logical step for the airline, and the claims it is denying. What remains uniquely Southwest is one fleet type, and a partially point-to-point map and a reputation for friendliness, and the fleet may be next. Jordan said Southwest is not building toward any one thing, but the addition of 11 lounges, a premium credit card, and first class tell a different story.

It’s a sea of sameness (to steal another industry trope.) Delta and United sell the same choices at similar prices, and Southwest sells them with a smaller map and limited international reach. Every concession after the bag fee was an admission that the discount model was not enough on its own, even though I personally believe that it was.

The numbers explain why management keeps moving the goalposts. In the third quarter, revenue per available seat mile jumped a record 21.6%, but capacity fell 0.4% and unit costs excluding fuel rose 3.8%. Capacity for 2026 is up only about 1.2%. That mix tells me growth came from price and fees, not from flying more, but in order to keep up that frothy pace it will have to find new markets to enter.

Domestic markets are mature, and Southwest’s biggest rivals already own the best international partners. Southwest has partnerships with Icelandair, China Airlines, EVA Air and Turkish Airlines, and Jordan said international loyalty partnerships and partner redemption launch next spring (I can’t wait to see what the redemptions look like.) Partners only go so far, though. Will some EVA Air customers book connecting flights on Southwest for its customers, maybe, probably, but that’s not going to move the needle.

Alaska already saw this writing on the wall and made a move. The rest of the premium push doesn’t come close to working if Southwest customers still buy tickets to Rome on another carrier. Chicago Midway can technically accommodate a 787, as can Dallas Love, and at LAX and Baltimore that’s a very easy fit. But all of those markets have real competition from major carriers. Other Southwest hubs like Fort Lauderdale face serious challenges with nearby Miami, Houston Hobby against United at Intercontinental, Denver for both international carriers and United.

But Southwest wanted to assimilate the carrier to every other carrier so it has to compete with every other carrier too. And in that regard, I can’t see a lounge program as aggressive as 11 locations working without it. If the fleet was exclusively Airbus, the international case is easier with longer legs and a JetBlue-like model (though that’s not working out well for them at the moment and they already have premium cards, lounges, and international partners.)

Unless Southwest sees unprecedented growth from what it’s already doing – possible, but not probable – this is the last frontier. So why deny it? Doesn’t that indicate a lack of forward thinking?

I will take the other side of the denial. My prediction is that Southwest announces a widebody order or lease within eighteen months of the pilot contract opening, and that Jordan or his successor will explain it as a natural evolution of the brand. The pattern is set. Every sacred cow gets slain once the numbers demand it, and the statement of the day is always true until the day it is not. Travelers should treat Southwest’s promises the way they treat any airline’s, as information about the current quarter and not a commitment about the next one.
