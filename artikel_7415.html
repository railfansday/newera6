New York Times lawsuit accuses privacy-focused Brave of exploiting publishers' journalism while selling scraped content to AI companies.

The New York Times is suing the makers of Brave Browser for unauthorized copying of journalism and copyright infringement for allegedly scraping publisher content to sell to AI companies and enabling customers to bypass its paywall. Although Brave positions itself as a white company that benefits consumers, the lawsuit paints a picture of a black hat company that exploits the work of publishers.

Brave Browser and Brave Search enjoy a reputation as a white hat, privacy-first company that’s an alternative to companies like Google, Anthropic, and ChatGPT. The New York Times’ complaint shows that not only is Brave scraping and monetizing publisher content, but it’s also selling it to AI companies, painting a picture of a company that is more black hat, at least to publishers.

Brave Browser and Brave Search positions themselves as the “good guys” on the Internet by casting themselves as the solution to an Internet that’s broken by surveillance capitalism. It positions itself as the antidote to Google’s aggressive monetization of its own users.

Brave Search’s unique selling points are that it uses its own independent search index, it protects user’s privacy, and it does not track them across the Internet the way Google does. All of that is true.

The New York Times’ copyright infringement lawsuit against the makers of Brave Browser and Search Engine reveals a darker reality behind the gleaming “user-first” stance in which Brave is alleged to exploit the work of journalists without compensation.

The New York Times lawsuit is a challenge to an alternative business model that instead of exploiting its users the way Google does it exploits the work of publishers on the internet by selling access to that content to AI companies, and uses it to provide answers that compete with the publishers’ own websites.

Brave allegedly provides unauthorized copies of Times journalism through its Search API, allowing third parties to use that content without obtaining licenses from The Times. The content that Brave scrapes off publisher sites is alleged to be sold to AI companies which then can use it to train their large language models. That same content is also sold to be used by AI Chat as grounding content that’s surfaced through retrieval-augmented generation (RAG).

The complaint cites Brave’s own marketing of its Search API for AI training, grounding, and content extraction as evidence that Brave knowingly promotes uses that The Times considers infringing. This is part of the complaint’s allegation that Brave deliberately created its products to exploit copyrighted content by making it available to third parties, including creating features that bypass publishers’ paywalls.

The inference that can be made here is that if Brave is doing this to The New York Times, it’s likely doing it to other websites that publish content on the Internet, whether it’s a recipe site, a product review site, or any other  kind of website that earns income from showing advertising.

Overall, the New York Times lawsuit (PDF) paints a picture of browser, search, and AI companies as parasites who, if they’re not exploiting users’ privacy, they’re exploiting the rest of the Internet while depriving publishers of the ability to earn income from their content.
