NASA reveals a haunting skull hidden in Antarctica’s ice

📅 03 November 2026 | Sumber: Baca Asli

Located in the Weddell Sea, east of the Antarctic Peninsula, Vega Island has a particularly haunting appearance when viewed from above. Its rocky and icy features resemble a grinning skull seen from the side, seemingly gazing toward nearby Devil Island. But behind this unusual sight is a more serious story about the changing condition of Antarctica's glaciers.

NASA satellite images show the island during the beginning of Antarctic autumn and during spring. The images were captured by the OLI (Operational Land Imager) aboard Landsat 9 on March 22, 2025, and October 10, 2026, respectively. (Clouds obscured the island in March 2026.)

Several natural landmarks create the skull's distinctive appearance. A nunatak, which is a rocky peak rising above the surrounding ice, resembles an empty eye socket. A lateral moraine, a ridge of material left along the side of a glacier, outlines the mouth. Meanwhile, Bahía del Diablo (Devil’s Bay) Glacier forms what looks like the skull's lower jaw.

The two satellite views also reveal a striking difference in snow coverage. In March 2025, snow blanketed the icy terrain, consistent with March being the wettest month in the region. By October 2026, however, extensive sections of the island were free of snow, leaving darker glacier ice exposed.

Glaciologist Mauri Pelto of Nichols College explained that unusually warm conditions during the winter of 2026, especially in June, contributed to the unusually large areas of exposed ice observed in October. According to Pelto, the exposed surfaces on Vega Island and neighboring islands suggest that relatively little fresh snow accumulated over the winter. Some of that snow was also lost through sublimation, when ice turns directly into water vapor, and through strong winds that swept snow away.

“The winter season was no treat for these glaciers.”

The lack of snow could have important consequences as Antarctica moves into its warmer months. Fresh snow reflects much of the sunlight that reaches it, while darker, exposed ice absorbs more solar energy. As daylight increases and temperatures rise, these darker surfaces are likely to warm more quickly, potentially speeding up glacier melting.

Even a modest amount of new snowfall could temporarily slow that process by covering the exposed ice.

“Even a thin cover of new snow from a spring storm would be helpful in the short run,” Pelto said.

Continued melting could also gradually transform the island's unsettling appearance. The same ice and rock formations that give Vega Island its skull shape are vulnerable to changes as the glaciers retreat.

“When entombed in snow and ice, the skull is preserved. But when exposed to melt, it weathers away, which will lead to a changing jawline and expanding eye socket.”

Scientists have been studying Vega Island's glaciers for decades, and satellite observations have played an important role in documenting their condition.

One of the earliest detailed surveys appeared in a 1982 study that used early Landsat satellite data, aerial photography, and topographic maps to examine the island's ice cover. Researchers identified four ice caps, along with rocky cliffs extending above the ice and glaciers flowing outward from the ice caps. Altogether, they documented 29 glaciers across Vega Island.

One of these, Devil's Bay Glacier, has been monitored in detail since the Antarctic summer of 1998-1999. Measurements collected over that period show an overall reduction in the glacier's mass.

The changes are not limited to Devil's Bay Glacier. Satellite observations spanning recent decades reveal that other glaciers on Vega Island have also lost ice, exposing increasing amounts of the underlying bedrock.

Long before satellites began monitoring its ice, Vega Island played an unexpected role in one of Antarctica's dramatic early exploration stories.

The Swedish Antarctic Expedition of 1901-1903 traveled to the region to investigate the geography, geology, climate, and wildlife of the eastern Antarctic Peninsula and surrounding areas. What began as a scientific mission turned into a desperate struggle for survival.

During the expedition, the explorers became separated into three groups, each forced to endure the Antarctic winter in a different location. Their situation became even more dangerous when pack ice destroyed their ship, leaving them stranded in one of the world's most unforgiving environments.

Despite the ordeal, Vega Island eventually became the setting for a moment of hope. In spring 1903, two of the separated groups reunited at a location later named Cape Well-met, near what now resembles the “forehead” of the island's giant skull.

Not long afterward, an Argentinean expedition arrived and rescued the survivors, bringing their extraordinary Antarctic ordeal to an end.

Today, Vega Island remains a place where dramatic natural formations, a remarkable history of exploration, and the effects of a changing climate come together. Its ghostly skull may be one of Antarctica's most unusual sights, but the gradual transformation of the ice that shapes it tells an even more important story.
