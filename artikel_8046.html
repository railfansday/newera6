White Snakeroot: A Pesky Weed—or a Welcome Low-Maintenance Fall Bloomer?

”What are those things? Weeds?”asked an interested observer when I was rooting around in a garden bed at Alice Austen House, the Staten Island museum and gallery where I garden twice a week. “No,” I said, “they’re not weeds. They’re white snakeroot.” The name didn’t help. There are also resident snakes in the garden. “They look like weeds,” he said. His untended garden is full of them. It is also full of autumn-active pollinators.

Fall-blooming white snakeroot is a brightening froth of white that lights up gardens and parks just when nights begin to lengthen and birds gather to leave their summer residences. So let’s keep the plant, instead of yanking it. While it self-seeds readily, it can be managed.

White snakeroot (Ageratina altissima) is very adaptable—highly tolerant of dry conditions, flowering in shade and in sun, and not minding damp roots, either. It seems to be designed for a world shaken by climate chaos. It will look after itself if you let it, and is the native gift that keeps on giving. The best time to manage new seedlings is in mid-spring when they are easy to spot and pull.

Altissima, the species name, refers to the stature of the plant, which can reach heights of five feet, although one-to-three is more usual. Blend masses of white snakeroot with asters and boneset for fall-blooming thickets of flowers.
