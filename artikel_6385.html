Why people with ADHD struggle to stop watching short videos

📅 03 November 2026 | Sumber: Baca Asli

In a survey of 302 adults, people with ADHD more often lost track of time while using short-video apps, and felt regret and guilt afterward.

A short-video feed never ends. Swipe past one clip on TikTok, Instagram Reels or YouTube Shorts and the next one is already playing, chosen to match what you’ve watched before.

In a survey of 302 adults, people with ADHD said those picks matched what they’d opened the app for just as well as they did for people without ADHD.

What set them apart was everything else: more trouble stopping, more lost time, and more regret once the app was closed.

The problem, the researchers wrote, isn’t bad recommendations. It’s a feed with no built-in place to stop.

The work comes from Elisabeth Lex and colleagues at Graz University of Technology (TU Graz) in Austria.

The research was released as a preprint ahead of ASSETS 2026, a computing accessibility conference held in Portugal in late October.

Earlier studies have tied heavy social media use and short-video habits to attention problems, but few have put numbers on how these feeds affect people who have ADHD.

The team recruited 302 adults through Prolific, a website that pays people to take part in research.

Of those 150 participants, 65 reported a formal ADHD diagnosis, while 85 identified themselves as having ADHD. This allowed the researchers to compare responses between the two groups

Splitting the ADHD group this way let the team check whether a formal diagnosis changed the answers. Participants ranged from young adults to people in their 70s.

YouTube Shorts was the most popular app, ahead of Instagram Reels and TikTok.

Each person rated 15 statements about their own viewing on a scale of 1 to 7, such as “Once I start watching short-content videos, I find it difficult to stop.”

The whole questionnaire took a typical participant under five minutes.

Participants with ADHD agreed more strongly that they often watched longer than they meant to and found it hard to stop once they’d started.

Asked how hard it was to pull away from the feed, the typical person with ADHD answered 5 on the 7-point scale. The typical person without it answered 3.

People with ADHD were also more likely to say they lost track of time while watching. Researchers call that time blindness – losing the sense of how many minutes have gone by.

Lex’s team named the pattern the engagement trap.

“By the ‘engagement trap,’ we mean a situation in which (video) recommendations are, on the one hand, perceived as highly relevant, but, on the other hand, people find it very difficult to tear themselves away from the screen and consciously control their own behavior,” Lex said.

The differences continued after people closed the app. Participants with ADHD more often said they regretted the time they’d spent, felt it hadn’t been valuable, and had put off things they meant to do.

They also reported lower mood after a long session than before it, and more guilt.

The biggest gap of all was frustration with their own difficulty disengaging. Even so, the differences were modest – small to medium by standard statistical yardsticks.

The team found no statistically reliable difference between people whose ADHD had been diagnosed by a doctor and people who identified it themselves.

“Our findings show that recommender systems optimized for engagement place a disproportionate emotional and behavioral burden on people with ADHD,” Lex explained.

Earlier research from a team led by Thomas Mildner found that people with ADHD were better than others at spotting manipulative design on social media.

Lex’s team wrote that a short-video feed works differently. There’s no deceptive visual element to spot, only a stream with no end.

With nothing to tell a viewer when to quit, stopping depends on self-control and a sense of how much time has passed. Those are the same abilities ADHD tends to strain, the authors wrote.

This may contribute to hyperfocus, a state of intense concentration that can make it difficult to disengage without an outside cue.

The survey also described seven possible app features, none of them built yet. They ranged from a gentle usage reminder to a mode that turns off the feed entirely.

None of them would require people to step away from the apps altogether.

People in every group liked the ideas, giving a typical rating of 5 on the 7-point scale. A fixed number of videos per session and an enforced break after a set time scored highest.

“To redress this imbalance, we must move away from optimization focused purely on engagement towards human-centered design practices that explicitly support users’ sense of time and their intentional control,” Lex said.

Everyone reported on their own habits from memory, and no one’s screen time was measured.

People with ADHD may also experience more guilt about scrolling, potentially influencing how negatively they describe their viewing habits.

The researchers did not independently verify ADHD diagnoses or ask about medication use, which may have influenced participants’ ability to stop watching.

The authors want future studies to compare how long people think they watched with how long they really did, using recorded watch time.

Whether a fixed video limit or a forced break actually cuts viewing time will take working versions of those features to find out.

The full study has not been published in a journal yet and is available as a preprint on arXiv.
