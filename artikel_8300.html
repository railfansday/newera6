31,000 Miles, 28 Years: Karl Bushby’s Walk Around the World by the Numbers

📅 03 November 2026 | Sumber: Baca Asli

Karl Bushby walks through a park in Peterborough, England, on October 22, 2026, during the final leg of his 28-year Goliath Expedition. (Photo: Leon Neal/Getty Images)

On November 1, 1998, Karl Bushby left Punta Arenas, Chile, and began walking north. Ahead of him, the 29-year-old British former paratrooper pushed a modified golf trolley filled with supplies and gear that he dubbed “The Beast.” His goal: to walk as far as a person could in unbroken footsteps on Earth, without using anything but his own hands and feet to make forward progress. His planned route for what he called the Goliath Expedition would take him from the southernmost tip of South America to his hometown of Hull, England, a journey he estimated would take him about 12 years.

Exactly 28 years and one day later, Bushby arrived back in Hull’s Queen Victoria Square to cheering crowds at shortly before noon on November 2, capping off a journey that was much longer—and far more complicated—than he could have imagined when he began. Along the way, he navigated shifting ice floes across the Bering Strait and swam the Caspian Sea to dodge geopolitical tensions and the English Channel after Eurotunnel refused to let him walk through the service tunnels connecting France and the UK. Not all the obstacles were physical, either: Bushby decamped to Mexico to regroup after his sponsorships dried up in the wake of the 2008 financial crisis and spent 10 years crossing Russia, stymied by visa requirements and an entry ban. (He finally received the permission he needed in 2014 after walking 3,600 miles across the United States to Russia’s embassy.)

All of that added up to something larger than a hike—a journey that ended up taking almost exactly half of the 57-year-old’s life. (It demanded a commensurate sacrifice, and not just from him. Bushby has a son, now 36, who he’s only seen a handful of times since leaving, and a 12-year-old granddaughter he had never met until finishing his trek; he also had and lost two long-term romantic relationships during the journey.)

For all that, the man behind the Goliath Expedition isn’t inclined to brag. When Backpacker writer Bill Donahue caught up with Bushby in Mexico in 2012, at what would turn out to be the halfway point of his walk, Bushby downplayed his own achievements.

“I’m an average guy,” Bushby said. “I’m less than average. I’m an underachiever. All I’m doing is putting one foot in front of the other.”

Here’s what Bushby’s big walk looked like by the numbers.

The approximate distance that Bushby walked from Punta Arenas, Chile, to Hull, England. That’s equivalent to 14 thru-hikes of the Appalachian Trail, 1.25 times the circumference of the globe, or roughly 13 percent of the average distance from the Earth to the Moon. That distance took him through 30 countries, according to the Associated Press.

The total distance Bushby had to swim during his trip. That included roughly 179 miles across the Caspian Sea in September and October 2024 and 21 miles across the English Channel in October 2026. Bushby did both of those swims in stages, resting on a support boat at night and returning to the coordinates where he had gotten out of the water in the morning.

The amount of time Bushby spent in jail after crossing borders without authorization. That includes 18 days in Panamanian custody after he crossed the roadless Darién Gap from Colombia and 58 days in Russian detention following his crossing of the Bering Strait from Alaska.

Bushby’s average pace across his entire trip. While far slower than other long-distance hikers—the small group of people to have hiked 10,000 miles in a year all had to knock off roughly 28 miles per day—the legal and financial hurdles Bushby faced, as well as his refusal to use transportation to bypass complicated terrain, meant that making miles was significantly more difficult for him.

The number of carts that Bushby used to carry his gear during the Goliath Expedition. The first, the Beast, survived three years before breaking down in Nicaragua. He used its replacement, the Beast 2, for the remaining 25 years. In an interview with The Guardian, Bushby called the cart “my Wilson,” referring to Tom Hanks’ famous volleyball and conversation partner from Castaway.
