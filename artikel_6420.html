A fabricated documentary scene is responsible for the enduring urban legend that lemmings willingly throw themselves off steep cliffs

📅 03 November 2026 | Sumber: Baca Asli

Lemmings do not commit mass suicide. Discover how a staged 1958 Disney documentary spread the myth and what these Arctic rodents really do.

For decades, lemmings have stood for blind group behavior, rushing together toward a cliff edge and the water below. The small Arctic rodents do nothing of the kind.

Much of that picture comes from White Wilderness, a 1958 Disney nature film. Its famous lemming sequence was shot in Alberta, a landlocked Canadian province, with animals brought in from elsewhere.

State wildlife biologists in Alaska tell a far less dramatic story. Populations of these Arctic animals rise and crash in cycles, and crowded lemmings spread out in search of food, sometimes crossing rivers and lakes on the way.

White Wilderness belonged to the studio’s “True-Life Adventures” series of nature films. It went on to win the Academy Award for Best Documentary Feature at the 1959 ceremony.

In one segment, narrator Winston Hibler follows a column of lemmings on what he calls “a march that will take them to a strange destiny.”

Hibler sets the story in the lemmings’ “weird world of frozen chaos.”

He calls the animals “victims of an obsession,” driven onward until they reach the edge above the water.

“Yet over they go, casting themselves out bodily into space,” Hibler says as the lemmings tumble from the ledge.

The film’s lemming footage was not shot on the Arctic tundra where lemmings live. The filmmakers bought lemmings from Inuit children in Manitoba, the Alaska Department of Fish and Game reported in a 2003 article.

A few dozen of the animals were then filmed running on a snow-covered turntable.

For the climax, crew members threw the lemmings off a cliff, the agency’s article says. The “sea” below was actually a river, shot with tight framing.

An investigation by Canadian television producer Brian Vallee exposed the staging in the early 1980s, the state agency noted.

Disney historian Jim Korkis addressed the scene in a 2012 post for the Walt Disney Family Museum. He wrote that the crew reportedly acted without the knowledge or approval of Walt Disney and the studio.

“…jumping off cliffs into the sea is an urban myth that only becomes reality when a nature cameraman steps in to make it a reality,” Korkis wrote.

Another lemming legend has a longer history. From the eastern Canadian Arctic to western Alaska, a common story held that lemmings fell from the sky.

That legend may come from the way lemmings suddenly appear in spring during peak years, according to an Alaska wildlife notebook on lemmings.

Thomas McDonough was a state wildlife biologist with the department when he discussed the suicide story in 2003.

“It’s a complete urban legend,” McDonough said. “I think it blew out of proportion based on a Disney documentary in the ’50s, and that brought it to the mainstream,” he added.

“Disney had to have gotten that idea from somewhere,” McDonough noted.

He suggested that Disney likely confused dispersal with migration and embellished a kernel of truth.

Alaska has one true lemming, the brown lemming. Several close relatives, including the collared lemming and the northern bog lemming, are also commonly called lemmings.

True lemmings measure 4 to 5.5 inches (100 to 135 millimeters) long. In some areas, their numbers peak every three to five years.

When lemmings exhaust the local food supply, they disperse, as moose, beaver and many other animals do. “Actual migrations do not occur,” the state’s notebook states.

Lemmings can swim and will cross bodies of water in search of new ground. Sometimes they drown.

At a lake or river, dispersing lemmings may stop and build up along the shore before swimming across. One account from Barrow, Alaska, tells of lemmings drowning and piling up on the shore.

Mass dispersal has been observed most dramatically in the Scandinavian mountains, home of the Norwegian lemming.

A 15-year study in northeast Greenland followed collared lemmings and four of their predators from 1988 to 2002. The predators were the stoat, a type of weasel, plus the Arctic fox, snowy owl and long-tailed skua.

The team worked across about 29 square miles (75 square kilometers) of the Karup Valley. Its model reproduced the four-year lemming cycle seen there.

The 2003 analysis in Science traced it to a one-year lag in the stoat’s response to lemming numbers.

A 2008 study of Norwegian alpine rodents, mostly lemmings, linked their swings from 1970 to 1997 to winter weather and snow conditions.

Shifts in temperature and humidity change conditions under the snow, the authors suggest, and less regular peaks may spread as the climate changes.

Their model also matched the observed absence of rodent peak years in that habitat after 1994.

The information was obtained from the Alaska Department of Fish and Game.
