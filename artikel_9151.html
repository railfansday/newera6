Recently, I’ve written for Creative Bloq's sister site Digital Camera World about a run of photographic rediscoveries. For instance, F Holland Day’s 1898 series The Seven Words resurfaced in September. Then in October, we saw a newly found cache of photos that document the making of the 1926 horror film Faust. These antiquated forms of physical media sat untouched for a century or more, and yet remain totally viewable. Amazing, really.

Now, let's do a thought experiment. If you're old enough, picture the digital work you made 20 years ago. That might be a website design, assets for a CD-ROM, a vector illustration… whatever.

Ask yourself: could you locate and open those files today? If you’re anything like me, the honest answer is “mostly no”.

It's ironic, really. Because digital work has no tangible physical presence, we tend to think of it as eternal. In truth, though, it's kind of the opposite.

This March, I visited memory card manufacturer Lexar’s facilities in Shenzhen, China. And one moment there has stuck with me ever since.

In a press conference, the company's representative explained that the flash memory used in consumer memory cards and SSDs can start losing data within a year if you leave them in a drawer and don't power them up.

Lexar told me it’s developing a dedicated device to address this issue, but it's early days. In contrast, a print just sits there, being a print.

Meanwhile, if you're putting your work online, there's more bad news. A Pew Research Center study found that 38% of webpages that existed in 2013 were no longer accessible a decade later. Platforms are no safer. Back in 2019, MySpace admitted that a server migration had wiped photos, videos and music uploaded between 2003 and 2015: an estimated 50 million songs from 14 million artists.

Its advice to users was to keep their own backups. But wherever you back stuff up, basically, you're running the same risk. As the sudden closure of Sora highlighted, venture capitalists don't really care about protecting your stuff as much as you do.

There's also the problem of actually opening files in years to come. Built something in Flash in 2012? I doubt you'll be able to salvage it now, after Adobe shuttered the format in 2020, and that's just one example among many.

So what can we do? There are few basic rules we all should be following if we want to be able to access our work in 2030, 2040 and further ahead. The first is to follow the 3-2-1 rule. Keep three copies of anything important, on two different types of storage, with one stored offsite or in the cloud. It's not a foolproof method, but it does reduce the odds of losing stuff exponentially.

Secondly, every few months or so, power up your SSDs and memory cards, so firmware can do its job. Thirdly, export your final files to open formats like TIFF, PNG, SVG or PDF/A, which are more likely to be readable in decades to come.

Fourthly, treat platforms as shop windows, not storerooms. And finally, print your work. As long as you can avoid floods or fires, paper-based storage has a better track record than digital.

Is this seems a bit of a faff, I'd have to agree. But consider this. If future historians end up knowing more about 1926 than 2026, it won’t be for lack of material. It’ll be because we couldn't be bothered to look after it – and what will future generations think of us then?
