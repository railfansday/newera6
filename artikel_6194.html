Marriott’s Fees Jump 14% as Hotel Owners Seek a Bigger Cut

Marriott said that its talks with hotel owners who want a bigger cut of the pie have been "constructive." It's easier for everyone to stay polite about the fee math when profit growth has revived significantly.

Marriott’s fees are climbing just as some of its hotel owners push for a bigger share of them.

Gross fees rose 14% to $1.52 billion in the third quarter, helped by a larger hotel network and by co-branded credit card fees, which Chief Financial Officer Jen Mason said on a Monday earnings call were “expected to rise in the high 30% range” for the full year. A group of owners wrote to Marriott’s management earlier this year asking for a bigger share of loyalty income, citing the card fees in particular. Net income rose 17% to $790 million.

Strong results may also give Marriott more leverage in those talks. Worldwide revenue per available room (RevPAR) rose 6.5% year over year, far above the 3.5% to 4% the company had projected in August, and Mason cited “a solid improvement in hotel margins.”

The gains reached the hotels that had been struggling most. Limited-service hotels posted a third consecutive quarter of RevPAR gains, up more than 6%.

Marriott beat expectations in the third quarter, with worldwide RevPAR up 6.5% year over year against August guidance of 3.5% to 4%, gross fees up 14% to $1.52 billion, and net income up 17% to $790 million. The company raised its full-year RevPAR outlook to 4.5% to 5%, up from the 1.5% to 2.5% it forecast in February, and offered an early view that 2027 worldwide RevPAR could grow 3.5% to 4.5%. The results land in the middle of a negotiation with a group of owners who wrote to management earlier this year asking for a bigger share of loyalty income, citing co-branded credit card fees, which CFO Jen Mason said are expected to rise in the high 30% range for the full year. CEO Anthony Capuano described Marriott's approach to owners as incremental, pointing to concessions already announced — a lower owner contribution rate on loyalty spending, better reimbursement for redemption stays on high-demand nights, relaxed operating and renovation rules, and a rebate for some well-reviewed hotels — and did not rule out more. Incentive management fees rose 22% to $181 million, which Marriott cites as evidence that owner and brand economics move together. Meanwhile, net room growth held at 4.6%, Capuano called key money the "weapon of choice" in winning development deals, and conversions made up more than a third of signings. The strong quarter arrived a week after Capuano told staff Marriott would eliminate some corporate roles while creating others; neither executives nor analysts raised the restructuring on the call.
