Actor Keith McDermott on Robert Wilson’s Legacy and Ghostwriting His Memoir: “I Saw a Robert Wilson Play and It Changed My Life”

📅 03 November 2026 | Sumber: Baca Asli

Over the more than 50-year period of his career, Wilson was arguably the most produced American theater director in Europe, if not the world. His aesthetic style has permeated European theater and opera culture to the extent that few directors have remained uninfluenced by what is commonly referred to as the “Wilsonian” use of lighting, set design, and performance style. However, he is not well-known in his own country.

The Robert Wilson productions that have reached the United States are some of the most iconic stage works of the 20th and 21st century: Einstein on the Beach and Lohengrin at the Metropolitan Opera House and at least 10 full productions at Brooklyn Academy of Music. Even in New York, Wilson’s artistic home, and even among artists, a not uncommon response at the mention of his name is “Should I know who that is?”

However, there is another response from New Yorkers of my generation, those of us living here when the city was at the artistic peak of the ’60s and ’70s, when Balanchine oversaw the New York City Ballet, Joe Papp headed the Public Theater, Andy Warhol ran The Factory, and apartments were affordable. That response, my response, is “I saw a Robert Wilson play and it changed my life.”

In 1975, a friend offered me a free ticket to a Broadway play. The title was A Letter for Queen Victoria, and I assumed I was in for a fusty historical drama, but as a young actor pursuing a commercial career, I never passed up an opportunity to see a play, especially if it was free.

Robert Wilson's A Letter for Queen Victoria.

The play began before the houselights went down as a young man and woman in ordinary dress stood in front of the curtain on opposite sides of the stage slowly turning in place. Nothing about their slow, hypnotic movement suggested “performance” until the houselights began to dim. Still turning, they raised their hands from their sides. Their palms, pressed in turmeric, became a blur of orange as their revolutions increased in speed and the curtain rose. The cast included the director’s grandmother as Queen Victoria, and a neurodivergent teenager, Christopher Knowles, whose nonacting presence broke the fourth wall, as he shielded his eyes from the stage lights to gaze into the house.

Audience members left in droves. A woman shouted something about child abuse on her way to the exit. But I sat mesmerized as the play evolved through a series of scenes unconnected by narrative or plot, fantastic in design, exquisitely lit, and often extraordinarily slow. Those of us who stayed until the end leapt to our feet, applauding and cheering for the actors, call after call. I stumbled from the theater into the city night feeling as if I had fallen in love.

My enthusiasm for that play led to both a professional and personal involvement with Bob (as anyone who knows him calls him). As a performer, I have appeared in five Wilson productions in various parts of the world and understudied him in a one‐man show. As a writer, I published an article about his chair collection and a roman à clef in which a character based on Bob pretty much takes over the book. In addition to our working relationship, Bob, a fellow Texan, has been my friend for over 50 years, and early in 2023 he asked me to ghostwrite his memoir.

Our sessions began formally at the Watermill Center, “a laboratory for the arts and humanities” on the South Fork of Long Island, founded by Bob in 1992. He had an apartment there where he stayed when he was in town. Assuming we’d work through his life chronologically, I came armed with questions about his childhood in Waco, Texas, his parents, and his schooling. But later, transcribing our recorded sessions, I cringed at my attempts to get Bob back on topic as he segued into a tale about absentmindedly depositing his airline ticket into a mailbox, then trying to board the plane with the postcards he had meant to mail, or a harrowing memory of six weeks spent in a Greek prison for a bit of hash discovered by airport security. My interruptions only spoiled the spontaneous flow of Bob’s thoughts.

Bob was a storyteller. Everyone who knew and worked with him was familiar with his stories, often told in a regional Texas twang and often more than once – and occasionally with a remarkable addendum. For instance, I knew that in his 20s, while working as a recreational therapist at the Goldwater Memorial Hospital for the terminally ill, Bob had choreographed a “dance” for quadriplegic patients in iron lungs, tying Day-Glo strings to mouth sticks they used to turn book pages that connected to mobiles hung from the ceiling. But in the retelling, I learned that Bob, after attending an Otis Redding concert, had persuaded the singer to come to the ward to accompany his quadriplegic dancers.

Once we gave up the chronological approach and Bob, unburdened by the need to deliver “The Story of My Life,” began to think in terms of “Life Stories,” our process relaxed and stories flowed. In addition to scheduled sessions, I could collect them everywhere, because Bob told them everywhere: at dinner parties, in the car, over the coffee and English muffins I began to deliver early each morning, or on our daily golf cart trips around the Watermill grounds. I always met him armed with my tape recorder.

A story he told frequently, both publicly, in interviews, and privately, was about seeing a policeman about to strike a young boy and intervening. From the sounds the boy made, Bob realized he was deaf. The boy, Raymond Andrews, was diagnosed as ineducable, but Bob realized he simply relied on visual cues not words. At 27 Bob became Raymond’s legal guardian and enrolled him in a school for the deaf. Raymond flourished in the creative community Bob created at his Spring Street loft (in SoHo before it was SoHo). Using Raymond’s drawings and his own observations of the boy, Bob created Deafman Glance, a seven-hour silent opera that signaled his European renown.

Bob told many stories about Jessye Norman, but the one that always brought him and his listeners to tears was of the great singer attempting to get through a Winterreise concert just after 9/11. During the third song, Norman began to cry and had to stop singing. When she regained control, she stood for 10 minutes, motionless, in silence, while the audience wept.

Bob’s vast social network also included socialites and celebrities, and those stories are filled with irresistible gossip: Marlene Dietrich divulged the name of the only contemporary American performer she completely admired (spoiler alert: it was Tina Turner); Diana Vreeland swallowed vials of cod liver oil to act as a buffer for vodka before an evening out; Samuel Beckett claimed that his lead actress in Happy Days was so brilliant because she didn’t understand a word she said.

In June of 2025, Bob went to Lithuania to stage rehearsals of a new production and then to Ljubljana, Slovenia, to direct Tristan and Isolde. When he was away, he sent me emails with story topics for our next session: “Ask me about my boat trip in the Middle East with Helen de Rothschild,” “Ask me about Louis Malle offering me the lead in Pretty Baby.”

When Bob returned to Watermill in July, we were to meet for final interviews, although by that time, I had all the material needed for the book. Shortly before my travels, I received a message that Bob’s health had declined, and he would be unable to see me. He died a week later on July 31 in his Watermill apartment.

Months after Bob’s death, I was still listening to and transcribing our taped conversations. In one conversation, recorded when we were having dinner at an Indian restaurant, Bob was telling me a story about being dressed as Santa Claus and handing out presents in Bali, where he spent every Christmas. Suddenly, he stopped mid-sentence and after a short silence, said, “I love you, Keith.”

I love you too, Bob, you changed my life.

THE SPACE BEHIND THE FACE, a memoir by the visionary theater director and visual artist, Robert Wilson, is published by Penguin Press and arrives on November 3.
