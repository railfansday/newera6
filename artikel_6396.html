Your brain follows bad advice even when you know better

📅 03 November 2026 | Sumber: Baca Asli

A new study reveals why people continue to follow unreliable information even when they know it could be misleading.

Knowing that information is unreliable doesn’t necessarily stop us from believing it.

A new study found that even when people were warned that certain clues were no more accurate than a coin toss, they still allowed those clues to influence their decisions.

Even more surprisingly, participants recognized that the unreliable clues were influencing their decisions.

It shows that simply being aware of a bias doesn’t always stop it from affecting our choices.

Keiji Ota, an assistant professor at the University of Tsukuba in Japan, carried out the research with Lucie Charles of Queen Mary University of London. Both also work with the UCL Institute of Cognitive Neuroscience in London.

The pair tested people’s choices in a simple color-guessing game. It wasn’t a test of real news, but the findings raise questions about how much a warning label alone can do.

The study involved 140 adults in the United Kingdom, who played one of five versions of an online game.

In each round, a computer chose red or blue as the correct answer, and players scrolled at their own pace through six colored squares that predicted it.

A percentage above each square showed its chance of being right, so a 65% square gave the right answer about two-thirds of the time.

The instructions described a 50% square as “as reliable as if the square’s color had been determined by flipping a coin” and told players to ignore it.

Some clues were wrong more often than right. A 35% square could still help, but players needed to reverse its answer, much as they might do with advice from someone who usually lies.

After seeing all six clues, players chose a color and immediately learned whether they were right.

Each person played close to 300 rounds, without a time limit, while the five versions used different mixes of clues.

Players did fairly well overall, choosing the right color in 58% to 64% of rounds, depending on the version. Guessing alone would give an average of 50%.

A player who used the percentages perfectly would ignore every 50% square. Yet in all five versions, people chose blue more often when more of those random squares showed blue.

The researchers built a computer model of each person’s choices to measure the effect.

The model estimated that players treated these squares as though they were right about 54% to 62% of the time.

That was more trust than the clues deserved, but it rarely made much difference to scores. In only one version did people who relied more on the random clues perform noticeably worse.

A clue that’s wrong 65% of the time can tell you just as much as one that’s right 65% of the time. You just have to use the opposite answer, and players found that harder.

In the version with only reversed and random clues, volunteers got 58.2% of answers right. In a matching version with trustworthy clues, they reached 64.4%.

Players also gave the last clue more weight than the first, especially when most clues needed reversing.

The researchers suggest that earlier clues may have faded from memory as new ones appeared.

Mentally flipping each answer could add to that strain. The extra effort might leave people with less ability to remember the clues they had already seen.

In two versions, players answered a question after each choice about one randomly selected kind of clue. They moved a slider to show whether they had followed its color, ignored it, or chosen the opposite.

Their ratings matched patterns in their choices: when more squares of a given kind agreed with their final answer, they reported more influence from that kind.

The most informative clues drew the strongest ratings, whether players needed to follow or reverse them.

The random clues showed the same pattern, suggesting that participants could recognize how unreliable information affected their choices.

People who relied most on a kind of clue also generally felt most influenced by it, although this did not hold for every kind in both versions.

A second model compared how likely a player was to choose an answer with and without a clue.

The difference matched people’s ratings and was closely tied to confidence, according to the authors.

The researchers offer several possible explanations for the bias. Seeing the same color repeatedly may make it feel like the right answer, an idea psychologists call the mere exposure effect.

People may also expect whoever provides information to be trying to help. That expectation could make it difficult to accept that a clue contains nothing useful.

Or players may have turned to random clues when the useful ones left them almost evenly split between red and blue. In that case, a useless clue could serve as a tiebreaker.

Ignoring those clues might also take more work than it was worth. Since the bias barely hurt scores, the authors suggest that players had little to gain by resisting it.

Reliability labels already appear alongside social media posts, product reviews, and health advice. Research on whether they reduce misinformation has produced mixed results.

Some studies found that star ratings for news sources reduced people’s willingness to believe and share false stories.

A 2022 study, however, found little average improvement in the quality of news people read and no reduction in mistaken beliefs.

The volunteers’ awareness also differs from earlier research in which people often missed influences on their choices, including the order of search results.

Those studies used cues that weren’t spelled out, while this game clearly stated how reliable each clue was.

Colored squares are much simpler than real headlines, and each version of the game used only three reliability levels.

The slider used to measure people’s awareness is also new and needs testing with other wording, labels, and instructions.

The questions asked about kinds of clues rather than their place in the sequence. That means the study couldn’t show whether players noticed how much more they relied on the last clue.

Fact-checking labels aim to limit the influence of false information, but this experiment cannot tell us exactly how people respond to them online.

Still, the findings reveal something important about human decision-making: Knowing that information is unreliable doesn’t necessarily stop us from using it. Recognizing a bias and overcoming it may be two very different things.

The study is published in the journal PLOS Computational Biology.
