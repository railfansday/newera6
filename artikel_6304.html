Don’t Scan This Abroad: The QR Code Travel Scam Tourists Miss

📅 03 November 2026 | Sumber: Baca Asli

QR codes are everywhere when we travel now, on restaurant tables, parking meters, hotel signs, airports, train stations, and transit machines. This travel scam known as Quishing or QR code phishing is becoming the fastest growing travel scam warning out there. Most QR codes are legitimate, but scammers are using fake QR code stickers and fake payment pages to catch travellers when they are tired, rushed, or distracted.

QR code travel scam happens when a fake QR code sends travellers to a fraudulent website. The code may be placed over a real QR code on a parking meter, restaurant table, hotel notice, airport sign, or transit machine, or it may arrive by text or email pretending to be a fine, toll, ticket, or check-in request. A scammer does not always need to hack a system or damage a payment machine. Sometimes they print a fake QR code sticker and place it directly over a legitimate code.

From a quick glance, the sign still looks official. The logo may look familiar. The instructions may seem normal. The page that opens may copy the colours, layout, and branding of the real company.

You think you are paying for parking, ordering food, checking into a hotel, or settling a toll. Instead, criminals may be collecting your credit card number, password, phone number, licence plate, email address, or other personal information.

The FTC has also warned about fake traffic violation texts that begin with a QR code and try to scare people into paying a fake unpaid balance.

The FTC has warned that scammers hide harmful links in QR codes to steal personal information, including by placing fake QR codes over real ones on parking meters.

Many restaurants now use QR codes for menus, ordering, or payment. Most are legitimate, but travellers should still be cautious. This is the easiest travel scam for QR code phishing as everyone uses it.

A menu QR code should usually show a menu. Be careful if it asks for information it does not need, such as your passport number, banking details, hotel room number, or a password.

Before scanning a QR code on a restaurant table, look at the surface. Does it look printed into the menu or table sign, or does it look like a sticker placed on top? If it looks raised, crooked, bubbled, or mismatched, ask staff for a printed menu or the restaurant’s official website.

Parking is one of the easiest places for this scam to work because travellers are often rushed and confused.

You may not know the local parking app. You may be dealing with another language. You may be worried about getting a ticket. That pressure makes a “scan to pay” sticker feel helpful.

Before paying through a parking QR code, inspect the sticker, read the URL preview, and check whether the payment page matches the city, parking authority, or official app named on the machine.

If anything feels off, do not use the QR code. Type the official website yourself, use the official parking app, pay at the machine, or ask local staff for help.

Airports and transit stations are full of QR codes: Wi-Fi, directions, tickets, luggage storage, forms, lounge access, and service information. Travel scams often happen when travelers are in a hurry. If you need wifi, a taxi, some food, QR codes are often the easiest way to catch you off guard.

That does not mean every code is unsafe. It means you should slow down when a public QR code asks for payment, passwords, ID documents, or personal information.

The FBI warns that malicious QR codes can send people to fake sites, steal victim data, embed malware, or redirect payments. The FBI warns that criminals can tamper with QR codes to redirect payments, steal login and financial information, or send people to malicious websites.

The easiest way to avoid this scam is to build one habit before you scan a public QR code.

Do not scan just because a sign looks official. Public signs, parking meters, table cards, and posters can be tampered with.

If the message says you must pay immediately, avoid a fine, settle a toll, confirm a booking, or prevent a penalty, slow down. Urgency is one of the biggest warning signs.

Before your phone opens anything, look at the QR code itself.

We call this the scratch test. You are not damaging the sign. You are simply checking whether the QR code feels or looks different from the rest of the surface.

Most phones show a preview of the website before opening the page. Read it.

Look for misspellings, extra words, strange endings, random letters, shortened links, or a domain that does not match the company, hotel, restaurant, city, or attraction.

Do not rely only on the padlock symbol. A secure connection does not automatically mean the site is legitimate. The real question is whether you are on the correct website.

For payments, passwords, account logins, or identity information, the safest option is often to bypass the QR code.

Open your browser and type the official website yourself. Use an app you already installed from the official app store. Ask an employee for the correct payment method. Contact your hotel, airline, rental car company, or bank through the phone number on your booking or card — not through the number on a suspicious page.

First, do not panic. Scanning a QR code does not automatically mean your information has been stolen. The bigger risk usually begins when you enter payment information, type a password, download a file, or provide personal details.

Close the page immediately if the link looks wrong, the message creates urgency, or the site asks for information you did not expect to provide.

Do not download an unfamiliar app or file from a QR code.

If you entered credit card information, contact your bank or card issuer using the official number on the back of your card or through the official banking app.

If you entered a password, change it through the official website or app. If you reused that password anywhere else, change it there too.

Watch for follow-up messages. A scammer who has some of your information may later pretend to be your bank, hotel, airline, rental car company, or payment provider.

Report the fake QR code to the business, parking authority, hotel, airport, or local authority so they can remove it.

A QR code may be suspicious if it looks like a sticker placed over another code, has peeling edges, opens a strange URL, uses a shortened link, creates urgency, asks for information that does not make sense, or directs you away from the official website or app.

At a restaurant, a QR menu should not need your passport or banking password. At a parking meter, the payment page should match the city or parking authority. At a hotel, a check-in code should not send you to a random payment page outside the official booking system.
