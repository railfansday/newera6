Einstein had a packed schedule in Pittsburgh in 1934, but he made time for a group of amateur astronomers

📅 03 November 2026 | Sumber: Baca Asli

In 1934, Albert Einstein visited a Pittsburgh astronomy exhibit organized by a plumber, highlighting his respect for amateur scientists and their work.

In December 1934, Albert Einstein came to Pittsburgh with a tight schedule. One stop he made a point of keeping was an exhibition by the city’s amateur astronomers.

The group’s co-founder, Leo J. Scanlon, was a plumber. Four years earlier, he had built a backyard observatory topped with a homemade aluminum dome for about $428.

Einstein was in town to give a lecture. The amateurs had invited him to see their display at the Mellon Institute, in Pittsburgh’s Oakland neighborhood.

In June 1929, Scanlon, who lived from 1903 to 1999, and Chester B. Roe founded the Amateur Astronomers Association of Pittsburgh.

In 1930, Scanlon and a group of friends traveled to the Stellafane convention of amateur telescope makers, which drew just under 100 registered guests. He kept a 41-page journal of the trip.

Back home, Scanlon built the Valley View Observatory beside his house on Van Buren Street. He ordered about 415 square feet (38.6 square meters) of sheet aluminum for the dome.

Its curved panels, called gores, were joined with an external double-turned seam. On November 15, 1930, with finishing touches still to come, the dome was lifted into place.

A dedication followed on November 23. The observatory without its dome cost $250, and the dome itself cost $178, according to Scanlon’s journal entries shared by the Springfield Telescope Makers.

A plaque near the site calls Valley View the first known astronomical observatory with an aluminum dome. Its shiny metal dome became a model for later observatories worldwide, the plaque adds.

In 1934, a national science convention met in Pittsburgh, with exhibits in the unfinished Mellon Institute building. Scanlon collected telescope-making materials from amateurs around the country for a free booth there.

He also wrote to Einstein, saying that a visit would encourage amateurs. Einstein answered that he would come if his schedule allowed.

“It would be my pleasure to examine the exhibit of the Amateur Astronomers,” Einstein wrote.

On December 28, 1934, Einstein, author of the general theory of relativity, lectured at the Carnegie Institute of Technology, now Carnegie Mellon University. His topic was mass-energy equivalence, the idea summed up as E = mc².

“People clambered to see Einstein’s lecture because he was one of the world’s first rock-stars,” said Gregg Franklin, a physics professor at the university.

A foggy photo taken from a balcony of the Little Theater is the only known picture of Einstein with a variant of that equation.

After his lecture in a nearby hall, Einstein asked the audience where the astronomers were, according to the club’s account. A professional astronomer in the room stood up.

“No, no,” Einstein replied. “I want to meet with the amateur astronomers.”

He reached the booth with a group of 40 or 50 people and stayed about 20 minutes. The display held equipment, material samples and photographs from telescope builders in eight states.

Scanlon’s brother Frank was working at a noisy grinding table. Scanlon showed Einstein the Foucault knife-edge test, which reveals tiny bumps and hollows on a telescope mirror.

On a 6-inch (15-centimeter) mirror, Scanlon pressed five fingertips to the glass. The test then showed five “hills” about one wavelength of light high, raised by the heat of his hand.

“I’m a plumber,” Scanlon replied when Einstein asked about his profession near the end of the visit. “Good,” Einstein answered, before asking him where the bathroom was.

David Topper, a Pittsburgh native, is a historian of science at the University of Winnipeg and the author of several books about Einstein.

“He had a thing about amateurs, whom he respected as much as professionals,” Topper said.

Another club member photographed Scanlon thanking Einstein at the booth. Scanlon kept Einstein’s written reply to his invitation.

“It is a letter that I still treasure,” Scanlon recalled many years later.

Steve Hill, a citizen scientist, showed that he could map Jupiter’s atmosphere with special color filters and a backyard telescope.

He then joined Patrick Irwin at Oxford University, whose team had studied Jupiter’s clouds with the MUSE instrument on the Very Large Telescope.

Their combined study concluded that the upper clouds are most likely ammonium hydrosulfide mixed with smog, not frozen ammonia, the European Southern Observatory reported in January 2025.

Clouds of ammonia ice may form only occasionally, when strong vertical convection carries ammonia to the top of the atmosphere.

Dan Caselden, a volunteer with the Backyard Worlds: Planet 9 citizen science project, co-discovered the cold brown dwarf W1935, the American Museum of Natural History announced in April 2024.

The James Webb Space Telescope later detected methane emission from W1935, a possible sign of aurorae.
