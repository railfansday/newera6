When can a kid ride in the front seat?

Has your big kid started asking – or downright begging – to graduate to the front seat of the car finally? Most kids start pining for the coveted spot long before they are big enough to safely sit up front.

Of course,safetyis your number one concern, butas their endless pleas wear you down, you might be wondering:when can I let them sit up front? Is it really that big of a deal to keep them in the back? We’ve been there. For help, we reached out to childhood safety experts to understand why the AAP recommends waiting until at least the age of 13 before letting your child ride in the front seat.

While the AAP guidelines are clear, local and federal laws (or, rather, lack thereof) muddy the waters. Lia Tuso, a Child Passenger Safety Technician (CPST) based in Buffalo, New York, tells Moms.com, “Unfortunately, when it comes to child passenger safety, the laws are the bare minimum.” There are no federal laws in the U.S. prohibiting children from riding in the front seat, but there are a handful of state laws. Delaware, for example, requires children to be at least 12 years old and 65 inches tall to sit in the front seat, while California mandates that children must be eight years old and over 57 inches tall. And it’s not just the U.S. – other peer nations’ laws are surprisingly lax around the topic. Most state laws in Australia, for example, require children to be at least seven years old to ride shotgun.

Tuso does not mince words about why younger children in the front seat of a car is so dangerous, saying, “The frontal airbags are designed to protect adult male bodies, not pre-pubescent children. The frontal airbags deploy at about 200 mph and they are designed to make contact with the average-sized male adult's bones. They simply aren't designed to protect children who don't have the same bone strength as an adult.”

Corri Miller-Hobbs, a safety expert with the Children's Hospital of Richmond at VCU, agrees. She also oversees the Virginia chapter Safe Kids Worldwide and tells Moms.com, “[Keeping children under 13 in the back seat] places the child in a seating position that is further away from a frontal crash, the most common type of crash.”

Tuso points out that research performed by the Children's Hospital of Philadelphia shows that children who are exposed to frontal airbags are twice as likely to suffer injury than children riding in the back seat. “The injuries that are most frequently seen from children riding in the front seat are head injuries such as traumatic brain injury and superficial head trauma, thoracic/chest injuries, abdominal injuries, pelvic injuries, and orthopedic injuries such as long bone fractures,” Dr. Alexis Javier, a Pediatrician with Memorial Hermann Health System in Houston, Texas, tells Moms.com. Importantly, Miller-Hobbs adds, “Injuries vary and can depend on how close the child is sitting to the frontal airbag and whether they were correctly restrained with a child restraint or safety belt.”

Tuso recommends seeking guidance from Child Passenger Safety Technician for every car seat transition, saying that too often, “parents will schedule a car seat installation check with a CPST for their newborn, but don't finish the journey to the front seat.”

“Oftentimes we see kids sitting upfront, who should still not only be in the back seat, but also in a booster!” Tuso says, “Most children will need a booster until ten to 12 years old. So parents should first ask, has the child maxed out the height/weight limits on their current booster seat?”

In addition to being physically developed enough to be safe in the front seat, Tuso shares important guidelines to ensure the correct fit for an adult seatbelt on a child, called the Five-Step Test.

“Even if your child passes the seat belt fit test in one seating position in one vehicle, that doesn’t mean they will pass the test in all vehicles and seating positions… so be sure to perform the seat belt fit test in any vehicle and any seating position your child may utilize,” Miller-Hobbs says. She also advises moving the passenger seat as far back from the airbag as possible for your child.

Tuso adds that watching crash test videos together on YouTube can be an effective way to demonstrate the necessity for strict rules around riding up front. “There is a lot of peer pressure around this age, especially with school drop-offs, but reminding your child of the importance of riding safely will set them up for success... even when they're driving on their own!” she says.

“We understand caregivers and children are excited and proud when new milestones are achieved,” Miller-Hobbs adds, “However, moving out of a car seat or belt positioning booster seat into a seat belt system should not be a milestone that is rushed.” Tuso agrees, saying, “The safest place for children under 13 is the back seat. While growing up is inevitable, injuries are preventable... there's no rush to the front seat.”
