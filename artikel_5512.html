Tornadoes strike in every month: a 12-month U.S. calendar of where and why

📅 03 November 2026 | Sumber: Baca Asli

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Tornado season by month runs well beyond spring, and this 12-month calendar shows where twisters strike, why the peak shifts and which historic storms marked each month

Tornadoes are not confined to spring. They can strike the U.S. in any month, including the depths of winter. The country records roughly 1,200 tornadoes in an average year, according to NOAA. That total is spread across a calendar far longer than the April and May storms most people picture.

A tornado is a violently rotating column of air that touches the ground and is attached to a cloud base. The setup behind most tornadoes pairs warm, moist air near the surface with cooler, drier air above. Winds that change speed and direction with height add the spin. Where those ingredients line up shifts as the seasons turn.

That is why tornado season by month looks less like a single spike than a band of risk moving across the country. The Gulf Coast and Deep South are most active in the cooler months. The Southern Plains peak in late spring. May is the most active month on average across the U.S., per NOAA Storm Prediction Center climatology. By early to mid summer, the peak has moved to the Northern Plains and Upper Midwest. In autumn, a secondary season develops across the South and the Mississippi Valley.

Two regional nicknames describe this geography, though neither has official boundaries. Tornado Alley generally refers to the Great Plains, where late-spring storms build over open, flat country. Dixie Alley covers parts of the Deep South, where many tornadoes arrive in the cool season. Storms there can move quickly, and rain or trees can block the view of an approaching funnel.

Tornado strength is rated after the fact, based largely on the damage left behind. The Enhanced Fujita (EF) Scale runs from EF0 to EF5. It replaced the original Fujita scale in the U.S. on Feb. 1, 2007. Tornadoes before that date carry F ratings. Older storms in this guide that have a rating are listed on the F scale, while storms since 2007 use the EF scale.

The 12 slides that follow move through the year in order, from January to December. The sequence is a calendar, not a ranking. Each slide explains where tornadoes tend to form that month, the weather patterns behind them and at least one documented storm that shows the risk in practice.

January tornadoes concentrate in the Deep South and along the Gulf Coast. Warm, humid air from the Gulf of Mexico can surge north there and collide with strong winter storm systems.

Most of the rest of the country is usually too cold and dry in midwinter to support tornadoes. Tornado-producing thunderstorms need plenty of moisture near the ground. Northern states rarely have it in January. Alabama, Mississippi, Louisiana and their neighbors sit close enough to the Gulf to draw on that moisture quickly.

The setup typically begins when a low-pressure system crosses the central U.S. Southerly winds ahead of it pull Gulf air inland. A band of strong winds a few thousand feet above the ground, known as the low-level jet, often speeds that flow. Temperatures and dew points climb across the South, sometimes within a day. A cold front then pushes in from the west, and thunderstorms form along and ahead of that boundary.

The winter jet stream supplies another key ingredient. In January, the jet stream often dips far south, placing its fast upper-level winds over the Gulf states. Those winds create strong wind shear across the region. Shear helps thunderstorms rotate. Rotating thunderstorms, called supercells, produce most strong tornadoes.

Selma, Alabama, showed how serious a January tornado can be. An EF3 tornado struck the city on Jan. 12, 2023. Selma sits on the Alabama River in the state's Black Belt region, far from the Great Plains that many people associate with twisters.

Dixie Alley is the informal name for the tornado-prone stretch of the lower Mississippi Valley and the Southeast. Alabama and Mississippi sit near its center. The same winter low-pressure systems that fuel these storms can bring snow and ice to states farther north at the same time.

The National Weather Service uses Doppler radar to detect rotation inside thunderstorms. Forecasters can issue a tornado warning from that radar signature before anyone confirms a funnel on the ground.

February tornado risk stays centered on the South and the lower Mississippi and Tennessee valleys. The Super Tuesday outbreak of Feb. 5–6, 2008, showed how widely late-winter storms can spread that danger.

The outbreak hit Tennessee, Arkansas, Kentucky and Alabama over two days. It shares its name with Super Tuesday, the Feb. 5, 2008, date when many states held presidential primary contests. The storms came after the U.S. adopted the Enhanced Fujita Scale, so they carry EF ratings.

February sits in the heart of the cool season. Across much of the central and northern U.S., the ground and air remain cold. Farther south, mild and humid air can return quickly whenever winds shift to blow from the south.

The trigger is usually a strong cold front. These fronts sweep out of the Plains behind powerful low-pressure systems. Ahead of them, warmer air surges north. Where the two air masses collide, the colder, denser air forces the warm air upward. Thunderstorms can then build fast.

Winter fronts often travel at high speed, and that shapes the storms they produce. Many February events feature a squall line, a long, narrow band of storms along or just ahead of the front. Tornadoes in a squall line tend to spin up quickly and can be brief. Heavy rain often surrounds them and hides them from view.

Other setups produce discrete supercells. These are isolated, rotating thunderstorms that form ahead of the main line. Supercells are responsible for most strong, long-lived tornadoes. When supercells and a squall line develop in the same system, tornadoes can touch down across several states.

Timing adds to the risk. Late-winter systems can arrive during busy daytime and evening hours, when people are at work, at school or on the road. Fast-moving storms leave little time between an alert and the storm's arrival.

The two main alerts mean different things. A tornado watch means conditions favor tornadoes across a broad area. A tornado warning means a tornado is occurring or has been indicated by radar nearby. During a warning, the standard guidance is to move to an interior room on the lowest floor, away from windows.

March is when tornado risk starts spreading north from the Gulf Coast into the Mississippi and Ohio valleys and the lower Midwest. On March 18, 1925, the Tri-State Tornado killed 695 people across Missouri, Illinois and Indiana, making it the deadliest tornado in U.S. history.

The month sits at the turn of the seasons. The sun climbs higher each week, and the spring equinox arrives around March 20. Ground across the South warms quickly under that stronger sunlight. Farther north, snow cover and cold air can hang on well into the month.

That gap produces sharp temperature contrasts across the middle of the country. Storm systems draw energy from those contrasts. A strong low-pressure system crossing the central U.S. can pull warm, humid air north on its eastern side while colder air pushes in behind it. Severe thunderstorms tend to form along the boundary between the two.

The jet stream adds another ingredient. In March it still carries much of its winter strength and often runs across the central and southern states. Strong winds aloft help a thunderstorm organize and rotate, the setup that can produce a tornado.

The Deep South stays in its active cool-season pattern through the month. As March goes on, the zone where warm and cold air collide reaches farther into the Mississippi and Ohio valleys.

The Tri-State Tornado crossed parts of all three states in a single day. People in its path had no weather radar, no television and no public tornado warnings to rely on. Commercial radio broadcasting was only a few years old. Residents had no organized way to learn a tornado was approaching before it reached them.

The National Weather Service now issues tornado watches when conditions favor tornadoes and warnings when one is spotted or indicated by radar. Doppler radar can detect rotation inside a storm before a tornado touches down. Those tools give people time to reach shelter, ideally a basement or a windowless interior room on the lowest floor.

That shelter guidance applies in March as much as in any other month. Warm spells this time of year can bring severe storms to communities that saw little of them over the winter.

April is a month of large tornado outbreaks across the central and eastern U.S. The Super Outbreak of April 3–4, 1974, produced 148 tornadoes across 13 states and killed 315 people.

The outbreak came before the Enhanced Fujita Scale took effect. Its tornadoes therefore carry ratings on the original Fujita scale. They are written as F ratings, not EF ratings.

A tornado outbreak is a cluster of tornadoes produced by one large weather system. In April, a deep low-pressure system can drag a cold front across several states at once. Thunderstorms then form along and ahead of that front over a wide area. Many of them can rotate at the same time.

Mid-spring sets up the sharp contrasts that feed these systems. Cold, dry air from Canada still pushes south as winter fades. Warm, humid air from the Gulf of Mexico flows north as the season warms. Where the two meet, the lower atmosphere becomes unstable and air rises quickly.

The jet stream supplies the other key ingredient. In April it remains strong and often runs across the middle of the country. Fast winds high overhead, paired with southerly winds near the ground, give a storm's updraft the twist it needs to start rotating.

An outbreak can unfold over many hours. Storms often begin in the afternoon, when daytime heating adds energy to the lower atmosphere. As the low-pressure system tracks east, new storms can keep forming after dark. They can reach communities far from where the first tornadoes touched down.

When instability and wind shear overlap across several states, one weather system can produce tornadoes in places hundreds of miles apart. That is how a single event can reach 13 states. On a major outbreak day, tornado watches can stretch across several states at the same time.

April sits between the cool-season activity of the Deep South and the late-spring peak on the Southern Plains. Tornado risk this month covers a broad stretch of the country, from the Gulf Coast north into the Ohio Valley and the Midwest.

May is the most active tornado month in the U.S. on average, according to NOAA Storm Prediction Center climatology. It is also when the Southern Plains, the heart of Tornado Alley, reach their late-spring peak.

The May setup depends on three ingredients lining up over the same ground. Warm, humid air flows north from the Gulf of Mexico near the surface. Hot, dry air from the desert Southwest and the high terrain of northern Mexico rides over the top of it.

The third ingredient is the jet stream. In late spring it still crosses the Plains with considerable strength. That supplies wind shear, the change in wind speed and direction with height that lets a storm rotate.

The boundary where moist and dry air meet near the ground is called the dryline. On many spring afternoons it sits across western Texas, Oklahoma and Kansas. Storms often form along or just east of it as the day heats up.

The layer of warm, dry air aloft acts as a lid, which forecasters call a cap. It can hold back storms for hours while heat and moisture build underneath. When the cap weakens in one spot, a single storm can grow quickly.

Those isolated storms are often supercells. A supercell is a thunderstorm with a deep, persistently rotating updraft known as a mesocyclone. Supercells produce most of the strongest tornadoes. The open, relatively flat Plains give them long stretches of ground to cross with little to break them up.

Two EF5 tornadoes from the past two decades show what a May storm can do. On May 22, 2011, an EF5 tornado hit Joplin, Missouri, and killed 158 people. On May 20, 2013, another EF5 tornado struck Moore, Oklahoma, a suburb of Oklahoma City.

Joplin lies in the far southwest corner of Missouri, near the Kansas and Oklahoma borders. Its location shows that May tornado risk is not limited to Oklahoma and Kansas. Moore sits in central Oklahoma, squarely inside the region most associated with Tornado Alley.

The Storm Prediction Center, based in Norman, Oklahoma, issues daily convective outlooks and tornado watches for the whole country. Local National Weather Service offices issue the tornado warnings that tell residents a storm is imminent.

In June, tornado activity moves north into the Northern Plains and Upper Midwest, the regions that peak in early to mid summer. On June 16, 2014, two EF4 tornadoes struck near Pilger, Nebraska.

The northward move follows the jet stream. In spring, this band of strong upper-level winds often dips across the Southern Plains and the South. By June, it usually tracks across the northern U.S. and southern Canada. The wind shear that helps organize rotating storms travels north with it.

Moisture follows a similar path. Warm, humid air from the Gulf of Mexico pushes farther inland as summer builds. It can reach Nebraska, the Dakotas, Minnesota and Iowa. That moisture feeds storms well north of the Oklahoma and Texas stretch of Tornado Alley.

Daylight adds more fuel. The summer solstice falls around June 21. It gives the northern states their longest days of the year. Long hours of sunshine heat the ground and the air just above it. Warm air near the surface rises quickly once a storm begins to form.

When that heating overlaps with enough shear, supercells can develop. A supercell is a thunderstorm with a deep, rotating updraft called a mesocyclone. Most strong and violent tornadoes come from supercells.

The Pilger tornadoes touched down near the small town in northeastern Nebraska on the same afternoon. Both earned EF4 ratings, the second-highest category on the Enhanced Fujita Scale.

A powerful supercell can sometimes produce more than one tornado at the same time. Separate funnels may form under different parts of the storm's rotating circulation. Paired tornadoes like these are uncommon. They force forecasters and storm spotters to track more than one threat at once.

Timing shapes the June risk as well. Plains tornadoes tend to form in the late afternoon and early evening, after the day's heating has peaked. Long June evenings mean many of these storms arrive while some daylight remains.

Geography matters too. Much of the Northern Plains is open farmland, with small towns spread far apart. A tornado can cross miles of fields before reaching a community. For residents of these states, the most active part of the year arrives weeks after the Southern Plains peak has passed.

July tornado activity concentrates across the northern tier of the U.S. and the Great Lakes region. By then the Southern Plains have usually passed their late-spring peak. The Northern Plains and Upper Midwest are in their early to mid summer season.

That shift follows the jet stream. In midsummer, the strongest upper-level winds typically run far to the north, often near the Canadian border. Storms need both instability and wind shear to rotate. Heat and humidity supply plenty of instability across much of the country in July. Strong shear is harder to find, and it tends to stay close to the jet.

States such as North Dakota, Minnesota, Wisconsin and Michigan sit near that band of stronger winds. Across the southern half of the country, shear is usually too weak in July to support many rotating storms.

Midsummer thunderstorms often organize into large clusters or lines that can travel hundreds of miles. Tornadoes can form along the leading edge of these systems. They tend to be brief. Heavy rain often hides them from view. The main hazard from these storm complexes is frequently damaging straight-line wind rather than tornadoes.

The Teton–Yellowstone tornado of July 21, 1987, showed that elevation offers no guaranteed protection. It struck the mountains of northwestern Wyoming and climbed above 10,000 feet. That makes it a rare high-elevation tornado. It was rated F4 on the original Fujita scale, which was still the U.S. standard in 1987.

Mountain tornadoes are uncommon. Cooler, thinner air at altitude usually limits the warm, moist conditions that storms feed on. The 1987 event is a reminder that the pattern has exceptions. Rivers, lakes, valleys and hills do not reliably deflect tornadoes either.

July also puts more people outdoors. Campers, hikers and boaters in parks and on lakes can be far from sturdy shelter when storms develop. For travelers in the northern U.S., checking the forecast before heading into remote areas is a practical step during this month.

August tornadoes are generally less frequent than spring twisters, and they can occur well outside the Plains. On Aug. 8, 2007, an EF2 tornado struck Brooklyn, New York, showing that late-summer storms can bring damaging tornadoes to dense urban areas.

The atmosphere in August works differently than it does in April or May. The jet stream typically sits far to the north in late summer. That position removes much of the strong wind shear that helps spring storms rotate. Heat and humidity remain plentiful, but the twisting ingredient is often weaker.

Many August thunderstorms are driven mostly by daytime heating instead. They tend to build in the afternoon and bring heavy rain, lightning and gusty winds. They often fade by evening. Tornadoes from these storms are often brief and weak. Some form along boundaries left by earlier storms, where colliding pools of air can spin up short-lived rotation.

The Brooklyn tornado also shows that a city offers no built-in protection. A common myth holds that tall buildings, rivers or urban heat keep tornadoes away. Large cities cover a small share of the land. Direct hits are therefore less frequent, but not impossible. When a tornado does cross a dense neighborhood, far more homes, cars and people sit in its path.

August falls within the Atlantic hurricane season, which runs from June 1 to Nov. 30. Landfalling tropical systems can add tornado risk near the coast in late summer.

The basic precautions do not change by season. A tornado watch means conditions are favorable for tornadoes to develop. A watch usually covers a broad area for several hours. A tornado warning means one has been spotted or indicated on radar. A warning targets a much smaller area and calls for immediate action.

People under a warning should move to a basement or an interior room on the lowest floor, away from windows. In apartment buildings without basements, an interior hallway or stairwell on a low floor offers more protection than upper stories.

September tornadoes often come from landfalling hurricanes and tropical storms rather than from classic Plains supercells. On Sept. 1, 2021, the remnants of Hurricane Ida produced an EF3 tornado in Mullica Hill, New Jersey, far from where the storm first came ashore.

Tropical systems are a recognized source of tornadoes. September also falls in the busiest stretch of the Atlantic hurricane season. Together, those two facts give the month a tornado pattern unlike spring's. The threat follows the track of each storm instead of staying in a fixed alley on the map.

The process starts with wind shear near the ground. As a hurricane moves over land, friction slows the winds at the surface. Winds a few thousand feet up stay much stronger. That sharp change in speed and direction over a short height creates the low-level spin a tornado needs.

The rotation usually develops in the outer rain bands, not near the eye. Small, shallow thunderstorms within those bands can become miniature supercells. The right-front quadrant of a tropical system, relative to its direction of travel, is the area most associated with tornadoes. For a storm moving north, that zone sits northeast of the center.

These tornadoes tend to differ from Plains tornadoes in several ways. Many are brief and rate low on the EF Scale, though the Mullica Hill tornado shows that stronger ones do occur. They are often wrapped in heavy rain, which can hide them from view. Their parent storms are low-topped. That can make the rotation harder to detect on radar, and warnings may come with little lead time.

The risk does not end when a hurricane loses strength. A weakening tropical system can carry moisture and shear hundreds of miles inland. Ida had weakened well below hurricane strength by the time its remnants reached New Jersey.

Tropical tornado threats often arrive alongside flash flooding and damaging straight-line wind. That mix can complicate decisions about shelter. A basement offers protection from a tornado, but low-lying spaces can also take on floodwater.

September tornadoes are not exclusively tropical. Cold fronts crossing the Plains and Midwest can still trigger ordinary severe thunderstorms. But storms tied to tropical systems make the month's risk highly mobile, with potential reach from the Gulf Coast to the Mid-Atlantic and Northeast.

October opens the autumn secondary tornado season across the South and the Mississippi Valley. An EF3 tornado that hit Dallas on Oct. 20, 2019, showed that a major city can face a strong twister months after the spring peak has passed.

A secondary season is a second, smaller rise in tornado activity that follows the summer lull. It does not match spring in scale. It does bring the threat back to southern states that see relatively few tornadoes in July and August.

The jet stream drives the shift. In summer, the strongest upper-level winds sit far to the north, near the Canadian border. As autumn arrives and northern latitudes cool, the temperature contrast between polar and tropical air sharpens. The jet stream strengthens and drifts back south over the central and southern U.S.

That southward move matters because the jet stream supplies the wind shear storms need. Strong shear lets a thunderstorm organize and rotate. Without it, a storm's rain-cooled downdraft tends to choke off the warm air feeding it, and the storm collapses.

Cold fronts return in October as well. Through much of the summer, fronts tend to stall or weaken before reaching the Gulf Coast. In the fall, stronger fronts push south out of Canada. They run into air near the ground that is still warm and humid.

The Gulf of Mexico helps keep that air humid. Its waters hold summer heat into the autumn. Southerly winds flowing off the Gulf can carry that moisture inland ahead of an approaching front.

When a vigorous jet stream and a sharp front arrive over that moisture, the ingredients for severe storms come together again. The pattern resembles a smaller version of spring, arriving at the other end of the year.

North Texas lies at the southern edge of the Plains, a region usually associated with spring tornadoes. The 2019 Dallas tornado shows that the same area can see significant tornadoes in the fall. A tornado in a dense metro area also puts far more homes, businesses and roads in its potential path than one crossing open farmland.

November tornadoes can reach well north of the Gulf Coast. Strong cool-season storm systems can push severe weather into the Midwest and Ohio Valley, as an EF4 tornado showed when it struck Washington, Illinois, on Nov. 17, 2013.

EF4 is the second-highest rating on the Enhanced Fujita Scale. A tornado that strong in mid-November shows that a late-autumn setup can produce a violent twister far from the Southern Plains, where spring activity peaks.

The fuel for these storms comes from contrast. By November, cold air pushing south from Canada grows sharper. Warm, humid air can still linger over the South. When a deep low-pressure system forms between the two, its winds can draw that moisture north into states such as Illinois, Indiana, Ohio and Kentucky.

The atmosphere in these setups usually looks different from a classic spring day. Shorter days and a lower sun limit surface heating, so instability tends to be modest. Wind shear, the change in wind speed and direction with height, can be very strong. Meteorologists call this a high-shear, low-instability environment, often shortened to HSLC. In spring, the balance often tips the other way, with plentiful instability supplying the energy.

Strong shear helps storms rotate even when they lack the explosive updrafts of a May afternoon. That combination makes forecasting harder. Storms may look less imposing on radar or in the sky, yet still organize enough to produce tornadoes. Forecasters weigh shear, moisture and the strength of the lifting mechanism, such as a cold front, to judge the threat.

The same powerful winds aloft move storms quickly. A fast-moving cell can cover a long distance in a short time. That shrinks the window between a warning and a tornado's arrival for anyone in its path.

Severe weather in the Midwest is most often associated with spring and summer. A November tornado falls outside that window. Shelter plans and alert sources, such as weather radios and phone warnings, apply in every month of the year.

December tornadoes are less common than spring twisters, but they can be deadly, and they often strike at night. An EF4 tornado struck Mayfield, Kentucky, on Dec. 10, 2021. That storm showed a December system can produce a violent tornado.

Winter tornadoes form under different conditions than late-spring storms over the Plains. Short days limit how much the sun warms the ground. That leaves the atmosphere with less of the buoyant energy that builds towering thunderstorms.

December often makes up for it with wind. The jet stream sits farther south in winter and blows harder. Strong low-pressure systems riding along it can pull warm, humid air north from the Gulf of Mexico ahead of a cold front.

When that moisture arrives beneath winds that change direction with height, storms can rotate even without intense heating. That rotation does not depend on daytime sunshine. The threat can last well after dark.

Overnight, a band of fast winds a few thousand feet above the ground, known as the low-level jet, often strengthens over the southern and central U.S. It can feed more Gulf moisture north and increase the turning of winds near the surface. Both help sustain rotating storms at night.

The Mayfield tornado hit Kentucky, hundreds of miles from the Gulf Coast. Strong December systems can push warm, unstable air well inland, carrying tornado risk beyond the Deep South.

Darkness adds a separate layer of risk. At night, a tornado may be visible only in flashes of lightning. Many people are asleep when overnight storms arrive.

Outdoor warning sirens are built to alert people who are outside. They are not designed to wake someone in a closed house. A NOAA Weather Radio with an alarm, or emergency alerts on a phone, can reach people that sirens miss.

Shelter guidance does not change with the season. The safest spot in a house is an interior room on the lowest floor, away from windows. Mobile and manufactured homes offer little protection. Residents of those homes need a plan to reach a sturdier building before storms arrive.

December also bridges two calendar years. The same cool-season pattern carries into January. Tornado risk across the South does not pause for the holidays.
